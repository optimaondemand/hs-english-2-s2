Building a Feature
Writing · Week 8
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
An investigative feature answers a question with evidence and shows its work.
The structure carries the argument: a lede that draws the reader in, a nut graf that states the question, sections that each hold evidence, a fair counterargument, and a close.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can name the parts of an investigative feature and the job each part does.
2
I can choose multimedia elements that carry evidence and not decoration.
3
I can cite sources, images, and the novel in MLA format.
📚 PART 1 · THE STRUCTURE

A news report states what happened. An essay argues from reasoning. An investigative feature does both with sources: it poses a question, gathers evidence from several kinds of material, and argues an answer a reader can check.

📝 ANATOMY OF A FEATURE
1
Headline and dek
A short title and a one-sentence line below it that names the question.
2
Lede
The opening scene or detail. It draws the reader in and points toward the question.
3
Nut graf
One paragraph that states the question, why it matters, and the answer the feature will argue.
4
Sections
Each section makes one claim, with evidence and a source. A subhead names the claim.
5
Counterargument
The strongest opposing answer, stated fairly and then answered.
6
Close
A final paragraph that returns to the lede and states what the evidence changed.
📚 PART 2 · A MODEL OPENING

A short model uses only facts from the novel. The question is what a self-made fortune buys at the end of a life.

✎ ON THE BOARD · A LEDE AND A NUT GRAF
At three o’clock the minister looked at his watch. The servants stood waiting in the hall, and no one else came.
Lede: a scene with concrete detail. It raises the question without stating it.
The man who gave West Egg its most famous parties was buried before his father, his neighbor, a few servants, a postman, and one stranger. What does a self-made fortune buy, and what does it leave unbought? The evidence suggests it buys attention while the money lasts and almost nothing after.
Nut graf: the question, why it matters, and the answer to be argued.
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A feature on this novel’s question draws on a text, a photograph, and a table of numbers. Which would a reader most likely trust without checking?
📚 PART 3 · MULTIMEDIA THAT CARRIES EVIDENCE

Multimedia in a feature is evidence. Each element should do work a paragraph cannot do as well: show a place, display a pattern in numbers, let a voice be heard.

ElementCarries evidence whenIs decoration when
Captioned imageThe caption states what the image shows, who made it, and which claim it supports.It sits beside the text with no caption and no connection to a claim.
Chart or tableAxes, units, and source are labeled, and the text names what the numbers show.It repeats a number already in the text, or its source is missing.
Audio or video clipThe speaker, date, and the moment to listen for are named, with a link to the source.It is attached for atmosphere and never discussed.

Use images and recordings that are in the public domain or openly licensed, or link to them at the source, and always give the credit. An item found online without a clear license is linked, not copied.

📚 PART 4 · CITING IN MLA

Every claim in a feature points to a source, and the page shows which one. MLA format uses a short in-text citation that leads to a full entry in the Works Cited list.

CaseIn-text citationNote
Novel read as an e-text with no page numbers(Fitzgerald, ch. 9)Name the chapter, abbreviated, when the text has no pages.
Article with page numbers(Author page)Author's last name and the page, with no comma between them.
Source with no author(“Short Title” page)Use the first words of the title, in quotation marks for an article.
Image or chartCaption: Fig. 1. Description. Credit.Label, description, and creator or source under the image.

The in-text citation comes before the period of the sentence: Nick judges Tom and Daisy harshly (Fitzgerald, ch. 9). The full entries are built in the Works Cited list, one for each source cited.

⚡ TRY IT · BUILD THE FEATURE
Choose the best answer.
1. A writer opens a feature with a general sentence about how the American dream has always fascinated people. What is the weakness?
2. A section makes a claim about the novel’s ending and then adds a photograph of a mansion with no caption. How should the writer revise?
3. A feature quotes Nick’s judgment of Tom and Daisy from an e-text with no page numbers. Which in-text citation is correct?
KEY TERMS
Lede: the opening scene or detail. Nut graf: the paragraph that states the question and answer. Dek: the line under a headline. Caption: text that says what a figure shows and where it came from.
