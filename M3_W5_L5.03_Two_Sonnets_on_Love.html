Two Sonnets on Love
Literature · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
One sonnet promises a beloved will not fade. The other refuses to flatter.
The Shakespearean sonnet is a compact argument: three quatrains and a couplet, with a turn. Sonnets 18 and 130 use the same form to opposite ends and show how layers of meaning and ambiguity operate inside fourteen lines.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify the rhyme scheme, the three quatrains, the couplet, and the volta in a Shakespearean sonnet.
2
I can explain how Sonnets 18 and 130 use the same form to opposite ends.
3
I can describe two readings that Sonnet 130 supports.
📖 READING
▶
Read Sonnets 18 and 130 below. Full text of the sonnets: Gutenberg eBook #1041.
📚 PART 1 · SONNET 18
Shall I compare thee to a summer’s day?
Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
And summer’s lease hath all too short a date:
Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
And often is his gold complexion dimm’d,
And every fair from fair sometime declines,
By chance, or nature’s changing course untrimm’d:
But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow’st,
Nor shall death brag thou wander’st in his shade,
When in eternal lines to time thou grow’st,
So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,
So long lives this, and this gives life to thee.
Shakespeare, Sonnet 18 (public domain)

The speaker offers a comparison, then rejects it because the beloved is better than summer: more lovely and more temperate. The rest of the poem lists what is wrong with summer (rough winds, a short lease, too much heat, dimmed gold) and then promises that the beloved will not decline. The last line says how: the poem itself keeps the beloved alive.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Sonnet 18 claims that the poem gives life to its subject. What kind of claim is that?
📚 PART 2 · SONNET 130
My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun;
Coral is far more red, than her lips red:
If snow be white, why then her breasts are dun;
If hairs be wires, black wires grow on her head.
I have seen roses damask’d, red and white,
But no such roses see I in her cheeks;
And in some perfumes is there more delight
Than in the breath that from my mistress reeks.
I love to hear her speak, yet well I know
That music hath a far more pleasing sound:
I grant I never saw a goddess go;
My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground:
And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare,
As any she belied with false compare.
Shakespeare, Sonnet 130 (public domain)

Sonnet 130 begins as the opposite of Sonnet 18. Each line takes a stock comparison of love poetry and refuses it: eyes like the sun, lips like coral, skin like snow, hair like gold. The list continues for twelve lines. The couplet then changes the direction of the poem.

📚 PART 3 · FORM: THE SHAKESPEAREAN SONNET

A Shakespearean sonnet has fourteen lines, usually in iambic pentameter: ten syllables to a line, in five pairs with the stress on the second syllable of each pair. The first line of Sonnet 18 scans as shall I | com-PARE | thee TO | a SUM | mer’s DAY. The lines fall into three quatrains (units of four lines) rhyming ABAB, CDCD, EFEF, and a closing couplet rhyming GG. The volta is the turn, the place where the argument shifts.

📚 PART 4 · LAYERS AND AMBIGUITY IN SONNET 130

Two readings are supported at once. In the first, the poem is a joke at the expense of poets who describe their beloveds with clichés. In the second, the poem is a sincere love poem that praises its subject by refusing to inflate her. The couplet decides between them only partly: it says the speaker’s love is rare, a word that means both excellent and uncommon, and that other women are belied, misrepresented, by false compare. Other words carry more than one sense. Reeks could mean “rises as a vapor,” the common sense in Shakespeare’s time, or carry the modern sense of a bad smell; both readings fit. Dun means a dull brownish gray, which is neither the white of snow nor an insult beyond the comparison.

The poem has a connection to the play. In Act 1 Helena says “Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind;” The speaker of Sonnet 130 looks with his eyes, sees a woman who is not a goddess, and loves her regardless. The two statements treat love as something that does not depend on what the eyes report.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE STRUCTURE DO?
For each item, choose the reading the text supports.
1. Where does the argument of Sonnet 18 turn?
2. What does the couplet of Sonnet 130 do?
💪 TAKEAWAY

A sonnet argues in stages and turns once. Sonnet 18 turns at line 9 toward permanence; Sonnet 130 holds its turn until the couplet and reverses the list that came before. In 130, ambiguity keeps both a joke and a love poem in play.
