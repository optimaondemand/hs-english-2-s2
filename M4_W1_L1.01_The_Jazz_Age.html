The Jazz Age
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The novel is set in the summer of 1922. The decade around it changed money, law, cities, and the question of who belonged.
Fitzgerald popularized the name the Jazz Age for the 1920s. A handful of well-established facts about those years explain much of what Chapter 1 takes for granted.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can name four changes that shaped American life in the 1920s.
2
I can connect a historical fact to a detail in the novel’s opening chapter.
3
I can separate what a novel reflects about its time from what it invents.
📋 READING
No reading is required for this lesson. Chapter 1 is read in the lessons that follow.
📚 PART 1 · A NEW SHAPE OF LIFE

The First World War ended in 1918. The next decade brought rapid change in how Americans lived and spent. Mass production made the automobile common well beyond the wealthy. Commercial radio broadcasting began in 1920, and sound films arrived late in the decade. The 1920 census found, for the first time, that a majority of Americans lived in towns and cities rather than in the country.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Which kind of change leaves the deepest mark on a community?

Nick Carraway, the narrator, is a product of these shifts. He leaves the Middle West for New York and the bond business:

Instead of being the warm centre of the world, the Middle West now seemed like the ragged edge of the universe—so I decided to go East and learn the bond business. Everybody I knew was in the bond business, so I supposed it could support one more single man.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The detail is historical as well as personal. Young men moved to cities, and securities and credit were a growing business. The novel treats the market as an ordinary career.

📚 PART 2 · PROHIBITION

The Eighteenth Amendment banned the manufacture, sale, and transport of intoxicating liquor. It took effect in January 1920 and was repealed by the Twenty-first Amendment in December 1933. Enforcement was uneven. An illegal trade supplied alcohol: bootleggers delivered it, and hidden bars called speakeasies served it. Organized crime grew rich on the business.

The effect on ordinary social life shows up at a dinner table in Chapter 1. No one hides the wine:

“You make me feel uncivilized, Daisy,” I confessed on my second glass of corky but rather impressive claret.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

Under a law that banned the sale of liquor, the wine still reaches a respectable table. The detail marks the age, and later chapters return to where such money comes from.

📚 PART 3 · WHO BELONGED

Cities grew with people moving in. Many Black Americans left the rural South for northern cities, a movement later called the Great Migration, and neighborhoods such as Harlem became centers of art and music. Immigrants from southern and eastern Europe also arrived in large numbers. Congress responded with the Emergency Quota Act of 1921 and the Immigration Act of 1924, which limited immigration by national origin.

These debates were part of everyday talk, and Fitzgerald puts a version of them in Tom Buchanan’s mouth at dinner. Tom cites a book that Fitzgerald invented as a stand-in for real bestsellers of the day on racial theory, among them Lothrop Stoddard’s The Rising Tide of Color (1920):

“Well, it’s a fine book, and everybody ought to read it. The idea is if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged. It’s all scientific stuff; it’s been proved.”
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The novel does not endorse the idea. The narration notes how little confidence lies under it: a man whose complacency is failing him reaches for borrowed theory. The remark places Tom in his decade and shows what he fears to lose.

📚 PART 4 · THE AUTHOR AND THE NOVEL
📝 THE DECADE ON ONE LINE
1920
Prohibition takes effect. The Nineteenth Amendment secures women’s right to vote.
1921
Emergency Quota Act limits immigration by national origin.
1922
Summer of the novel’s events. Fitzgerald publishes Tales of the Jazz Age.
1924
Immigration Act sets permanent national-origin quotas.
1925
The Great Gatsby is published in April.
1929
Stock market crash in October.
1933
Prohibition is repealed.

F. Scott Fitzgerald was born in St. Paul, Minnesota, in 1896 and attended Princeton. His first novel, This Side of Paradise (1920), made him famous young, and he married Zelda Sayre the same year. In 1922 he popularized the era’s name in the title of a story collection, Tales of the Jazz Age. He lived for a time on Long Island, in the North Shore communities that are commonly taken as models for the novel’s two Eggs. The Great Gatsby appeared in 1925 and sold modestly compared with his earlier books. Fitzgerald died in 1940. The novel’s reputation grew in the decades after his death.

A novel written in 1925 about 1922 looks at its own moment from a few steps away. The facts above explain the setting. They do not settle what the novel thinks of it.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE DETAIL
Choose the best reading of each detail from Chapter 1.
1. Tom warns that the white race will be swamped by other races. How is this remark best read?
2. Nick notes that everybody he knew was in the bond business. Which feature of the decade does this detail place?
🔑 KEY FACTS
Prohibition: a national ban on liquor, 1920 to 1933, widely evaded.
Immigration quotas: laws of 1921 and 1924 limiting arrivals by national origin.
Setting versus publication: the story takes place in 1922; the book appeared in 1925.
