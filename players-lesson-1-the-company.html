Three kinds of speech
Verse, rhymed couplets, and prose
●
Shakespeare writes in three kinds of speech, and the kind a character uses tells you who is speaking. Blank verse is unrhymed lines of about ten syllables and five beats. A rhymed couplet is two lines whose last words rhyme. Prose is ordinary speech with no line pattern.
BLANK VERSE
Now, fair Hippolyta, our nuptial hour
Draws on apace; four happy days bring in
Another moon;
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.1 (public domain), Theseus
A RHYMED COUPLET
Thou speak’st aright;
I am that merry wanderer of the night.
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 2.1 (public domain), Puck
PROSE
A very good piece of work, I assure you, and a merry.
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.2 (public domain), Bottom

Count the beats
Mark a line, then find where it bends
●
A weak syllable followed by a strong one is one foot. Five feet make a line of iambic pentameter. Mark weak syllables x and strong ones /. Say the line aloud and the strong syllables are the ones you lean on.
A LINE, SCANNED
The course of true love never did run smooth

course/
ofx
true/
lovex

did/

smooth/
Five feet: the-COURSE, of-TRUE, love-NEV, er-DID, run-SMOOTH. Ten syllables, five beats, no rhyme: blank verse.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
How many strong beats does The course of true love never did run smooth carry?
NOW YOU SCAN
Love looks not with the eyes, but with the mind;
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 1.1 (public domain), Helena
Type the line, and write the five strong syllables in capital letters
For example: the COURSE of TRUE love NEV-er DID run SMOOTH.
●
One common reading is: love LOOKS not WITH the EYES but WITH the MIND. The word Love also wants a strong beat. If you give it one, the first foot flips to strong then weak, and the meter bends at the start of the line. Poets bend the meter on purpose, to make a word stand out.
Which word does the bend make stand out?
One word.

Quick Write 17: Four Lines of Verse
Week 1 · 10 points · turned in as this week’s PDF
●
This is the whole Quick Write 17 assignment. Write it in the box at the end of this section. Your teacher scores this box.
THE ASSIGNMENT

Write 4 to 6 lines of unrhymed verse about a modern conflict between a family rule and a personal choice, then add a note of 100 to 150 words that names one place where your meter bends on purpose and says why.

●
Focus question. Where can a line break its own rhythm, and what does the break make a reader notice?
STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Name the conflict in one sentence: a rule your family keeps (a curfew, a planned career, a rule about friends) set against a choice you want to make. The conflict can be invented.
2
Draft 4 to 6 lines of blank verse: about ten syllables and five beats a line, no rhyme. Say each line aloud and tap the beats.
3
Make one line inverted. Put its words in an unusual order for emphasis, as Titania does in Out of this wood do not desire to go.
4
Write the note: copy the line where the meter bends on purpose, say where the bend falls, and explain what it makes a reader notice.
5
Use at least two of this week’s Word Study words, used accurately.
SUCCESS CRITERIA (YOUR CHECKLIST)
☐
The 4 to 6 lines are unrhymed, run about ten syllables, and carry about five beats each.
☐
The lines make a family rule and a personal choice clear.
☐
One line has an inverted word order.
☐
The note names one deliberate bend in the meter and explains what it makes a reader notice.
☐
At least two Word Study words are used accurately, and the note is 100 to 150 words.
HOW IT’S SCORED
Criteria Full credit Partial Pts
Blank verse lines
4 pts		 Four to six unrhymed lines of about five beats each that make the rule and the choice clear. Fewer than four lines, rhymed lines, or a rhythm or conflict that is hard to follow. 4
Note on the meter
4 pts		 Names one deliberate bend, quotes the line, and explains what the bend makes a reader notice. Names no bend, or says only that the line sounds good. 4
Craft targets
2 pts		 One inverted line and two Word Study words used accurately; the note is 100 to 150 words. Fewer than two of either, or off the length target. 2
Total: 10 pts

What a script fixes, and what it leaves open
The director’s space
●
A script fixes the words, their order, and who speaks. It leaves open how fast, how loud, where people stand, and what the set looks like. A director works in the open space. Some stage directions sit in the text itself, and those are fixed too.
A STAGE DIRECTION IN THE TEXT
Enter Oberon at one door, with his Train, and Titania at another, with hers.
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 2.1 (public domain), stage direction before the quarrel
The text fixesThe director decides
The words. Ill met by moonlight, proud Titania.The tone. A threat, a tease, or a weary greeting.
The order. Oberon speaks first and Titania answers.The distance. How far apart the two stand, and who moves.
The entrances. They come in at different doors.The pace and the pauses. How fast the quarrel runs.

Quick Write 18: What the Text Specifies
Week 2 · 10 points · turned in as this week’s PDF
●
This is the whole Quick Write 18 assignment. Write it in the box at the end of this section. The notes above are your raw material. Your teacher scores this box.
THE ASSIGNMENT

Write 200 to 300 words about the scene you chose in the Choose your scene tab. In the first paragraph, state what the words fix: who speaks, in what order, and any stage direction or line that commands an action. In the second, state what the words leave open for a director to decide. In a short third paragraph, explain why the scene matters to the play as a whole.

●
Focus question. What can a director not change in this scene, what must a director decide, and why does the scene matter to the whole play?
STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Name your scene by act, scene, and title, and name its speakers.
2
List what the words fix: the lines, their order, who says them, and any stage direction in the text. Give two examples.
3
List what the words leave open: tone, pace, gesture, distance between speakers, set, and cuts. Give two examples that matter to this scene.
4
Write one short paragraph on why this scene matters to the play as a whole.
5
Use at least two prepositional or participial phrases to add detail, and at least two Word Study words, used accurately.
SUCCESS CRITERIA (YOUR CHECKLIST)
☐
The scene is named, and two things the words fix are stated with specific examples.
☐
Two things the words leave open are stated and tied to this scene.
☐
One paragraph explains why the scene matters to the whole play.
☐
At least two prepositional or participial phrases add detail.
☐
At least two Word Study words are used accurately, and the writing is 200 to 300 words.
HOW IT’S SCORED
Criteria Full credit Partial Pts
What the text fixes and leaves open
4 pts		 Names two fixed features and two open choices, each with a specific example from the scene. Fixed and open features are mixed up, general, or missing examples. 4
Why the scene matters
4 pts		 A clear paragraph connects the scene to the play’s central events or ideas. The paragraph retells the scene or stays general. 4
Craft targets
2 pts		 Two prepositional or participial phrases and two Word Study words used accurately; length is on target. Fewer than two of either, or off the length target. 2
Total: 10 pts
Your Quick Write 18, in full
200 to 300 words. Join your notes into three paragraphs.
