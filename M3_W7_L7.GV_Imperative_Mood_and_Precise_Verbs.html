Imperative Mood and Precise Verbs
Grammar + Word Study · Week 7
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A command has no stated subject, and its strength comes from its verb.
The imperative mood gives a command, a request, or a direction. Its subject, you, is understood. Specific verbs make the direction exact.
🎯 OBJECTIVES

2

📚 PART 1 · THE IMPERATIVE MOOD

A verb in the imperative mood takes its base form and has you as an understood subject. The speaker’s name or title, when it appears, is a word of direct address and is not the subject. Three kinds of imperative appear in this week’s reading.

✎ ON THE BOARD · IMPERATIVES IN THE PLAY
Go, Philostrate, Stir up the Athenian youth to merriments; Awake the pert and nimble spirit of mirth; Turn melancholy forth to funerals
Four imperatives: go, stir up, awake, turn. Philostrate is addressed. The subject of each verb is you (understood).
Trip away. Make no stay;
A negative imperative: make no stay. Oberon’s order to the fairies is a command built from short, strong verbs.
Out of this wood do not desire to go.
A negative imperative with do not. Titania commands Bottom and does not request.
Speak, Pyramus. Thisbe, stand forth.
Two imperatives, each with a name of direct address. Pyramus and Thisbe are the people spoken to, not the subjects.

An imperative sentence ends with a period or, for force, an exclamation point. It differs from a statement about obligation (You must go) in that the command is the sentence itself.

📚 PART 2 · PRECISE VERBS

Weak verbs such as go, do, put, get, make, look, and say leave the actual action to guess. Titania’s order to her fairies starts general and becomes exact in the next lines.

Be kind and courteous to this gentleman;
Hop in his walks and gambol in his eyes;
Feed him with apricocks and dewberries,
With purple grapes, green figs, and mulberries;
The honey-bags steal from the humble-bees,
Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 3.1 (public domain)

Be kind and courteous names a quality. Hop, gambol, feed, and steal name actions a fairy can perform. A direction becomes followable when each verb names one action.

📚 PRACTICE · REVISE THE DIRECTION

Revise each vague direction into precise imperatives. A model revision opens below each item.

Go over there and do the wall.
Model: Cross to center, raise two fingers, and hold them until Pyramus finishes his speech.
Say your lines better.
Model: Slow the final line and lower your volume on the last word.
Put the props where they go.
Model: Set the lantern on the table, hang the thorn-bush on the left post, and lay the mantle under the chair.
📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words appear in this week’s scenes and in the theater vocabulary of the play. Learn the sense the play gives each word.

cue (noun): the words or action that signal an actor to speak or move
Root: possibly from the Latin quando, “when”, abbreviated in actors’ scripts (origin uncertain). In the play: Your cue is past; it is ‘never tire.’
properties (noun): the movable objects used on stage in a play; props
Root: Latin proprietas, from proprius (one’s own). In the play: I will draw a bill of properties, such as our play wants.
extempore (adverb): without preparation; on the spur of the moment
Root: Latin ex tempore, “out of the time”. In the play: You may do it extempore, for it is nothing but roaring.
interlude (noun): a short entertainment performed between larger events
Root: Latin inter (between) and ludus (play). In the play: to play in our interlude before the Duke and Duchess
prologue (noun): a speech that introduces a play before the action begins
Root: Greek pro (before) and logos (speech). In the play: Write me a prologue
con (verb): to study closely in order to learn by heart
Root: Old English cunnan, “to know”. In the play: to con them by tomorrow night
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

Some words arrive in English unchanged and keep a clipped, working life in the theater.

ad lib (from Latin ad libitum, “at pleasure”): to speak or perform without a script, inventing the words in the moment
A performer who forgets a line and invents a replacement ad libs. Quince tells Snug he may play the lion extempore, which invites the same freedom. Example: “The actor ad-libbed a joke when the prop door stuck.”
