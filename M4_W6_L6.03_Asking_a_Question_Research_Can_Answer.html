Writing and Research · Week 6
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A research question names what evidence could settle. It is neither a matter of opinion nor a single fact.
The lesson covers how to judge a question, how to narrow it as sources come in, and how the structure of an informational source helps a researcher read it.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can distinguish broad, focused, opinion, and single-fact questions.
2
I can refine a question using what the first sources show.
3
I can identify six text structures and the features of an informational source (headings, captions, charts).
📚 PART 1 · WHAT MAKES A QUESTION RESEARCHABLE

A researchable question can be answered by evidence from sources, and the answer is not obvious in advance. Four kinds of question look similar and behave differently.

🔍 FOUR KINDS OF QUESTION
Single fact: When was The Great Gatsby published?
One source settles it. Nothing is left to research.
Opinion: Was Gatsby a good man?
Sources can inform it, but it is settled by judgment, not evidence alone.
Too broad: What was America like in the 1920s?
Any answer needs a book. The question gives no way to choose evidence.
Focused: How did advertising in the 1920s present the automobile to buyers?
It names a subject, a period, and a kind of evidence. Sources can answer it in several ways.

When a topic is interesting but very large, what helps most?
📚 PART 2 · NARROWING A QUESTION

A question is rarely right the first time. The first sources show whether it is too large or too small, and the question is adjusted. The usual adjustments are a place, a period, a group, or one aspect of the topic.

📝 REFINING A QUESTION AS SOURCES COME IN
1 · BROAD
What was the Jazz Age?
First search: too many results, no common thread.
2 · NARROWED BY SUBJECT AND PLACE
How did Prohibition affect everyday life in American cities?
Added: one law, one setting. Results now share a topic.
3 · FOCUSED
What does the evidence show about how speakeasies changed where urban Americans socialized between 1920 and 1933?
Added: one aspect, one period, a kind of evidence.

Two signs show that a question needs adjusting. If a search returns too many unrelated results, the question is too broad. If only one or two sources respond, the question may be too narrow, or the researcher may be using the wrong terms.

📚 PART 3 · TEXT STRUCTURES AND FEATURES

Informational sources organize ideas in recognizable structures. Knowing the structure helps a researcher predict where the answer sits and what each part does.

StructureWhat it doesSignal words
DescriptionDetails about a subject, often by parts or qualities.for example, such as, includes
Problem and solutionA difficulty is stated, and a response follows.problem, issue, solved by, one answer
ChronologicalEvents are told in the order they happened, as in a history.in 1920, later, by 1933
Compare and contrastTwo or more things are set side by side.unlike, both, whereas, similarly
Cause and effectA result is linked to what produced it.because, as a result, therefore
SequenceSteps in a process, in the order they must be done.first, next, then, finally

Chronological and sequence are easy to confuse. A chronological text reports events that happened once, in time order. A sequence text gives steps that someone is meant to repeat.

Features give a researcher shortcuts. Headings show the structure of the whole text. Captions identify what an image shows, and often when and where it was made. A chart carries a title, labeled axes, and a source line, and the source line says who gathered the data.

⚡ TRY IT · QUESTIONS AND STRUCTURES
Choose the best answer.
1. Which research question is focused enough to answer in a short feature?
2. A first search for the word Prohibition returns thousands of unrelated results. What is the best next step?
3. “When the river rose, the bridge closed. As a result, buses were rerouted, and the market lost a week of customers.” Which structure does the passage use?
4. “Unlike the old library, which kept paper records, the new library stores its records digitally.” Which structure does the sentence use?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Focused question
A question that names a subject, a period, and a kind of evidence.
Text structure
The pattern a source uses to organize its ideas, such as cause and effect.
Source line
The note under a chart that says who gathered the data.
