📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The story of Daisy and Gatsby arrives in Nick’s voice, from Jordan’s mouth, about events that neither of them was part of. The structure is a set of nested tellings.
Most of Chapter 4 is told by a character inside Nick’s story. Each layer adds knowledge and adds a possibility of distortion.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe the layers of narration in Chapter 4 and who speaks in each.
2
I can summarize Jordan’s account of Daisy in 1917 and of her wedding.
3
I can explain what nested narration gains and what it risks.
📋 READING
Reread Jordan’s account in Chapter 4, from the October day in 1917 through the drive in Central Park.
📚 PART 1 · THE FRAME
📝 WHO IS TELLING, AND WHAT CAN THEY KNOW
FITZGERALD, THE AUTHOR
NICK, RETELLING THE SUMMER OF 1922 AFTER IT ENDED
Sees only what he saw and heard. Knows the ending.
JORDAN, AT THE PLAZA TEA-GARDEN, TELLING THE YEARS 1917 TO 1919
Was there for some of it. Was told the rest. Describes herself as half asleep when Daisy reacted to Gatsby’s name.
GATSBY AND DAISY. Their feelings reach the reader only through Jordan’s words, with Nick’s comments laid over them.

The account begins with a frame that places Jordan in time and space and reminds the reader who is reporting:

One October day in nineteen-seventeen—
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)
(said Jordan Baker that afternoon, sitting up very straight on a straight chair in the tea-garden at the Plaza Hotel)
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

The parenthesis carries the frame. Jordan’s words follow for several pages, and Nick appears only to mark where she stopped. The reader hears Jordan, filtered through Nick’s memory of Jordan, and has already been told in Chapter 3 that she was dishonest:

She was incurably dishonest.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 3 (public domain)

Her account is vivid, specific, and detailed, and it comes from an unreliable teller. The two facts have to be held together.

📚 PART 2 · DAISY AT EIGHTEEN

Jordan remembers Daisy as the center of her town:

The largest of the banners and the largest of the lawns belonged to Daisy Fay’s house. She was just eighteen, two years older than me, and by far the most popular of all the young girls in Louisville. She dressed in white, and had a little white roadster, and all day long the telephone rang in her house
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

White dress, white roadster: the details echo the white dresses of Chapter 1. In the roadster that morning is a young officer whom Jordan does not know:

His name was Jay Gatsby, and I didn’t lay eyes on him again for over four years—even after I’d met him on Long Island I didn’t realize it was the same man.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A story reaches you through three people. What is the most important thing to ask?

Jordan has a reason to shape the story: she is acting for Gatsby, who has asked her to arrange a meeting. The warmth in her portrait of the young Daisy serves the request.

📚 PART 3 · THE WEDDING EVE

The most detailed scene is the evening before Daisy’s wedding, which Jordan saw herself as a bridesmaid:

In June she married Tom Buchanan of Chicago, with more pomp and circumstance than Louisville ever knew before.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)
I came into her room half an hour before the bridal dinner, and found her lying on her bed as lovely as the June night in her flowered dress—and as drunk as a monkey. She had a bottle of Sauterne in one hand and a letter in the other.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

Daisy is distressed. She hands over a string of pearls, a gift from Tom valued at a very large sum, and weeps. Jordan and a maid revive her, and the letter is left in the soap-dish, coming apart. Then the account turns:

Next day at five o’clock she married Tom Buchanan without so much as a shiver, and started off on a three months’ trip to the South Seas.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

Jordan reports a collapse and a recovery. The text does not say what the letter held, and the reader should note what is reported and what is guessed. A reader who supplies the writer of the letter has gone beyond the text.

📚 PART 4 · THE REQUEST AND NICK’S REPLY

Jordan reaches the point of her story. She tells Nick that Gatsby wants something small:

“Gatsby bought that house so that Daisy would be just across the bay.”
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)
“He wants to know,” continued Jordan, “if you’ll invite Daisy to your house some afternoon and then let him come over.”
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

Nick’s reaction takes over the narration and sets the story in a larger frame:

The modesty of the demand shook me. He had waited five years and bought a mansion where he dispensed starlight to casual moths—so that he could “come over” some afternoon to a stranger’s garden.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)
Then it had not been merely the stars to which he had aspired on that June night. He came alive to me, delivered suddenly from the womb of his purposeless splendour.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 4 (public domain)

The nested account does two jobs. It tells the history of Daisy and Gatsby, and it changes Nick’s view of the parties: the mansion and the music have been a single signal across the water. The chapter also keeps the facts separate from the pleading. Jordan tells one side, and Nick is moved by it.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE LAYERS
Choose the best reading for each question.
1. Why does Fitzgerald give Daisy’s past to Jordan and not to Nick?
2. On the wedding eve, Daisy holds a bottle and a letter and then marries Tom the next day. What can be said from the text?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Nested narration: a story told inside another story, by a character within the main narrator’s account.
Frame: the passage that introduces an embedded story and says who is telling it.
Witness and teller: someone who saw an event and someone who reports it, who may not be the same person.
