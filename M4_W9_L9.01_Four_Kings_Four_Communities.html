Four Kings, Four Communities
Literature · Week 9
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Four rulers, from an ancient epic to a 1925 novel, answer one question: what does a ruler owe a community?
Odysseus, Oedipus, Theseus with Oberon, and Gatsby rule in different ways over different communities. Read together, they show how authority, responsibility, and self-government change across periods and cultures.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can compare how four rulers relate to the communities they lead.
2
I can explain how each work’s period and culture shapes its answer.
3
I can state a position on what a ruler, and a self-governing person, owes a community.
📋 READING
No new reading. Review the four works: the Odyssey, Oedipus the King, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, and The Great Gatsby.
📚 PART 1 · ODYSSEUS AND ITHACA

Odysseus and Ithaca. The Odyssey sets a king’s absence against a household’s decay. In Ithaca, the suitors consume the wealth of the hall while Odysseus is away, and Telemachus and Penelope hold the house together without the king’s power. Odysseus returns as a beggar, tests who has stayed loyal, such as Eumaeus, and recovers his place in the contest of the bow. The community’s order depends on one man’s return and one man’s strength.

📚 PART 2 · OEDIPUS AND THEBES

Oedipus and Thebes. Oedipus the King opens with a community in suffering. A priest describes the plague:

“A blight is on our harvest in the ear, / A blight upon the grazing flocks and herds, / A blight on wives in travail; and withal / Armed with his blazing torch the God of Plague / Hath swooped upon our city emptying / The house of Cadmus, and the murky realm / Of Pluto is full fed with groans and tears.”
The Priest, trans. F. Storr, public domain

Oedipus answers as a ruler who owes the city a cure, and he binds himself by his own order:

“Let no man in this land, whereof I hold / The sovereign rule, harbor or speak to him; / Give him no part in prayer or sacrifice / Or lustral rites, but hound him from your homes.”
Oedipus, trans. F. Storr, public domain

The source of the plague is the ruler himself. The play ends with the chorus’s warning to every citizen:

“Look ye, countrymen and Thebans, this is Oedipus the great, / He who knew the Sphinx’s riddle and was mightiest in our state. / Who of all our townsmen gazed not on his fame with envious eyes? / Now, in what a sea of troubles sunk and overwhelmed he lies! / Therefore wait to see life’s ending ere thou count one mortal blest; / Wait till free from pain and sorrow he has gained his final rest.”
the Chorus, trans. F. Storr, public domain
📚 PART 3 · THESEUS, OBERON, AND ATHENS

Theseus, Oberon, and Athens. In A Midsummer Night’s Dream, two rulers govern two realms. Titania names her quarrel with Oberon as the cause of the disordered seasons:

And this same progeny of evils comes
From our debate, from our dissension;
We are their parents and original.
Titania, Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 2.1 (public domain)

In Athens, Theseus restores a different kind of order. In Act 1 he upheld Egeus’s claim against Hermia. In Act 4 he sets it aside:

Egeus, I will overbear your will;
For in the temple, by and by with us,
These couples shall eternally be knit.
Theseus, Shakespeare, A Midsummer Night’s Dream, 4.1 (public domain)

The ruler’s will, not the law he announced earlier, ends the conflict. The comedy rewards that will with marriages and a feast.

📚 PART 4 · GATSBY AND WEST EGG

Gatsby and West Egg. Gatsby holds no office. His authority is a fortune and an invented self, and the narrator names the invention:

The truth was that Jay Gatsby of West Egg, Long Island, sprang from his Platonic conception of himself.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 6 (public domain)

The community he gathers is a crowd of guests. When the parties end, it leaves:

But it wasn’t any use. Nobody came.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 9 (public domain)
📚 PART 5 · THE FOUR SIDE BY SIDE

The four cases set side by side:

RulerCommunitySource of the troubleHow the ruler answersHow it ends
OdysseusIthaca: household and islandSuitors consume the hall during the king's absence.Returns in disguise, tests loyalty, wins the bow contest.The house is restored by the king's return and force.
OedipusThebes: a city under plagueThe ruler's own unknowing act.Hunts the cause, then sentences himself.Creon takes charge; the chorus warns all citizens.
Theseus and OberonAthens and the fairy woodThe ruler's quarrel disorders the seasons; a father's claim threatens his daughter.Oberon undoes the enchantment; Theseus overrules Egeus.Order returns by the ruler's will, then a blessing.
GatsbyWest Egg: a crowd of guestsAn invented self built to win one person.Builds a house and a party to be seen.He dies; almost no one attends.

Three of the four rulers hold a crown or a command. Gatsby holds only a fortune and a public image, which makes him an American version of the self-made king: a ruler with no office and a community with no obligation to stay.

📚 PART 6 · WORLD LITERATURE, ONE QUESTION

The four works come from four periods and four cultures: ancient Greek epic, Greek tragedy, Early Modern English comedy, and an American novel of the 1920s. The question stays the same, and the answers shift.

The Greek works tie a community’s health to a ruler’s fate and to forces beyond either of them. Shakespeare’s comedy places order in a ruler’s decision and ends in celebration. Fitzgerald’s novel has no throne at all. The self-made man must earn a community from nothing and pays when it does not hold.

A self-governing person, in any of these worlds, owes a community what the rulers here struggle to give: honesty about the self, restraint in the use of power, and loyalty that outlasts the good times.

⚡ TRY IT · COMPARE THE RULERS
Choose the reading the texts best support.
1. What most separates Gatsby from the other three rulers?
2. Titania says the quarrel’s results come “From our debate, from our dissension; We are their parents and original.” Which other ruler’s situation does this line most resemble?
3. In Act 4, Theseus overrules Egeus. What does the scene suggest about how the comedy restores order?
KEY TERMS
World literature: works from different periods and cultures read for a shared question. Self-government: a person’s rule over his or her own conduct, which a community depends on. Ruler and community: the pairing each work tests.
