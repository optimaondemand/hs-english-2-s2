📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Matched ideas deserve matched grammar. Parallel structure puts equal ideas in the same form.
This lesson covers parallel structure in words, phrases, and clauses, using sentences from Chapters 1 to 4, with Word Study on words of self-invention.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can recognize parallel structure in a sentence and name the repeated form.
2
I can repair a sentence whose parts are not parallel.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · PARALLEL STRUCTURE

Parallel structure means that equal ideas in a series or a pair are written in the same grammatical form: noun with noun, verb phrase with verb phrase, clause with clause. Parallel parts make a sentence easy to follow and give it rhythm. A writer who breaks the pattern by accident makes the reader stumble. A writer who repeats a beginning on purpose, as repetition, makes the repetition carry emphasis.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PARALLEL SENTENCES IN THE NOVEL
I was within and without, simultaneously enchanted and repelled by the inexhaustible variety of life.
Two pairs: within and without; enchanted and repelled. Each pair matches in form, and the second pair mirrors the first as inside against outside.
Laughter is easier minute by minute, spilled with prodigality, tipped out at a cheerful word.
Two matching past participle phrases: spilled with prodigality, tipped out at a cheerful word. Both treat laughter as something poured.
Filled with faces dead and gone. Filled with friends gone now forever.
Repetition: each sentence opens with Filled with. The repeated opening builds feeling through accumulation.
I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever; I wanted no more riotous excursions with privileged glimpses into the human heart.
Parallel clauses: two statements that begin with I wanted. The semicolon joins them as equals.
People were not invited—they went there.
Balanced clauses of contrasting meaning: a negative and a positive, each a subject and a verb.

To repair a sentence, find the item that breaks the pattern and rewrite it to match. Compare: Gatsby is rich, mysterious, and has a great many parties. The first two items are adjectives, and the third is a verb phrase. A parallel version: Gatsby is rich, mysterious, and generous.

Practice. Write a sentence with a series of three items about a person from the novel, all in the same form. Write one pair of balanced clauses on the same subject.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Six words of self-invention from Chapter 4.

punctilious (adjective) · very careful about correct form and detail
Root: Italian puntiglio, a small point, from Latin punctum
In the text: This quality was continually breaking through his punctilious manner in the shape of restlessness.
incredulity (noun) · unwillingness or inability to believe
Root: Latin in-, not, + credere, to believe
In the text: My incredulity was submerged in fascination now
threadbare (adjective) · worn thin by use; stale from overuse
Root: Old English thread + bare, cloth worn down to the threads
In the text: The very phrases were worn so threadbare that they evoked no image
authentic (adjective) · genuine; being what it claims to be
Root: Greek authentikos, original, genuine
In the text: To my astonishment, the thing had an authentic look.
aspire (verb) · to hope and work toward a high goal
Root: Latin aspirare, to breathe toward, to strive for
In the text: Then it had not been merely the stars to which he had aspired on that June night.
elaborate (adjective) · worked out in great and careful detail
Root: Latin elaborare, to work out
In the text: you should have heard the elaborate way he worked up to it
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN
alter ego (Latin) · literally “other I”; a second self, or a different identity a person adopts
Example: In his stories the quiet clerk wrote as a bold explorer, an alter ego he never showed at work.
