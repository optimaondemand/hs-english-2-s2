Week 5
Two Readings of “All Men Are Created Equal”
Literature · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Two public figures appeal to the same sentence and draw opposite conclusions. The difference lies in what each says the words cover.
In 1858 Abraham Lincoln and Stephen A. Douglas, rivals for a seat in the United States Senate from Illinois, held seven joint debates; the first took place at Ottawa, Illinois, on August 21, 1858.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state each side’s reading of “all men are created equal” in one sentence.
2
I can evaluate whether each reading’s reasoning is sound, naming the premise and the evidence.
3
I can compare what each speaker claims and what each limits.
📋 READING
▶
Read the excerpts quoted below, from Douglas’s opening speech and Lincoln’s reply. The full debate is in Gutenberg eBook #48364. The 1858 language is quoted exactly.
📚 PART 1 · DOUGLAS’S READING

Douglas speaks first. He states his view of the government, then answers Lincoln’s use of the Declaration.

I believe this government was made on the white basis. I believe it was made by white men, for the benefit of white men and their posterity forever; and I am in favor of confining citizenship to white men, men of European birth and descent, instead of conferring it upon negroes, Indians, and other inferior races.
Douglas, First Joint Debate at Ottawa, August 21, 1858, in Noted Speeches of Abraham Lincoln (public domain)
Mr. Lincoln, following the example and lead of all the little Abolition orators who go around and lecture in the basements of schools and churches, reads from the Declaration of Independence that all men were created equal, and then asks, How can you deprive a negro of that equality which God and the Declaration of Independence award to him? He and they maintain that negro equality is guaranteed by the laws of God, and that it is asserted in the Declaration of Independence. If they think so, of course they have a right to say so, and so vote. I do not question Mr. Lincoln’s conscientious belief that the negro was made his equal, and hence is his brother; but, for my own part, I do not regard the negro as my equal, and positively deny that he is my brother or any kin to me whatever.
Douglas, First Joint Debate at Ottawa, August 21, 1858, in Noted Speeches of Abraham Lincoln (public domain)

The claim rests on the intent of the founders: the government was made by and for white men. The passage does not quote the Declaration’s wording in support. It states what the founders intended and treats the Declaration’s “all men” as an abolitionist reading.

I do not hold that, because the negro is our inferior, therefore he ought to be a slave. By no means can such a conclusion be drawn from what I have said. On the contrary, I hold that humanity and Christianity both require that the negro shall have and enjoy every right, every privilege, and every immunity consistent with the safety of the society in which he lives.
Douglas, First Joint Debate at Ottawa, August 21, 1858, in Noted Speeches of Abraham Lincoln (public domain)

Douglas then names who settles the matter: This is a question which each State and each Territory must decide for itself. His reading of the Declaration rests on this principle of local decision, often called popular sovereignty: the people of each State and Territory decide what rights the negro holds there.

Two people read the same sentence and disagree about its meaning. What most often explains it?
📚 PART 2 · LINCOLN’S READING

Lincoln replies. He reads “equal” as applying to specific rights, and he states the limits of his own position in the same passage.

I have no purpose to introduce political and social equality between the white and the black races. There is a physical difference between the two which, in my judgment, will probably forever forbid their living together upon the footing of perfect equality; and, inasmuch as it becomes a necessity that there must be a difference, I as well as Judge Douglas am in favor of the race to which I belong having the superior position. I have never said anything to the contrary, but I hold that, notwithstanding all this, there is no reason in the world why the negro is not entitled to all the natural rights enumerated in the Declaration of Independence,--the right to life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness. I hold that he is as much entitled to these as the white man. I agree with Judge Douglas he is not my equal in many respects,--certainly not in color, perhaps not in moral or intellectual endowment. But in the right to eat the bread, without the leave of anybody else, which his own hand earns, he is my equal and the equal of Judge Douglas, and the equal of every living man.
Lincoln, First Joint Debate at Ottawa, August 21, 1858, in Noted Speeches of Abraham Lincoln (public domain)

The reading separates equality in every respect, which Lincoln denies, from equality in certain rights, which he affirms. The rights are those the Declaration lists, life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness, with one more: the right to the fruit of one’s own labor. He also states that he has no purpose to introduce political and social equality.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT EACH READING CLAIMS

Both readings can be set side by side.

📝 SAME WORDS, TWO READINGS
DOUGLASLINCOLN
Who is coveredWhite men: men of European birth and descent.All men, including the negro, in the rights the Declaration lists.
What “equal” meansNot extended to the negro; rights allowed are those consistent with the safety of the society.Equal in some respects: life, liberty, the pursuit of happiness, the fruit of one’s labor.
Basis of the claimThe intent of the founders: made by white men, for the benefit of white men.The Declaration’s own list of natural rights.
Weak point to testThe sentence says “all men” with no qualifier; intent is asserted, not shown from the text.Whether the rights can stand while political and social equality is denied.
📚 PART 4 · ARE THE READINGS SOUND?

An argument is valid when its conclusion follows from its premises. It is sound when it is valid and the premises are true. Evaluation asks both questions.

Douglas’s reading depends on a premise about the founders’ intent. The premise is stated, not supported by a quotation, and the sentence itself says all men with no qualifier. A conclusion drawn from an unsupported premise is not sound, even if the reasoning from the premise is orderly.

Lincoln’s reading is valid: if the Declaration names rights and attaches them to all men, the conclusion that the negro has those rights follows. The premise is supported by the text. A further question remains. Lincoln states that he has no purpose to introduce political and social equality. Whether the rights he affirms can stand without those forms of equality is not settled by the passage, and a reader should say so when evaluating it.

⚡ TRY IT · EVALUATING THE READINGS
Choose the best answer.
1. Douglas says the government “was made on the white basis.” What support does he give in the passage?
2. Lincoln concedes that the negro is “not my equal in many respects.” What does the concession do?
3. Which comparison of the two speakers is accurate?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Valid
The conclusion follows from the premises.
Sound
The argument is valid and its premises are true.
Intent as a premise
A claim about what authors meant, which needs support from the text or the record.
