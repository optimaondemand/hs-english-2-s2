Appositives and Modifiers in Fitzgerald’s Sentences
Grammar + Vocab · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
An appositive renames a noun beside it. A modifier adds a quality. Fitzgerald stacks both to build a person or a place in a single sentence.
This lesson covers appositives and modifiers (adjectives and adverbs) in sentences from Chapter 1, with Word Study on words of wealth and class.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify an appositive and the noun it renames.
2
I can identify adjectives and adverbs and the words they modify.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · APPOSITIVES AND MODIFIERS

An appositive is a noun or noun phrase placed next to another noun to rename or explain it. It is usually set off by commas, and the sentence still works if it is removed. A modifier is a word or phrase that describes another word: an adjective describes a noun, and an adverb describes a verb, an adjective, or another adverb.

✎ ON THE BOARD · SENTENCES FROM CHAPTER 1
Only Gatsby, the man who gives his name to this book, was exempt from my reaction
Appositive: the man who gives his name to this book. It renames Gatsby and supplies a fact in one phrase. Remove it, and the sentence still says Gatsby was exempt.
His speaking voice, a gruff husky tenor, added to the impression of fractiousness he conveyed.
Appositive: a gruff husky tenor renames the voice. The two adjectives gruff and husky stack to give the sound.
Now he was a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner.
Modifiers: sturdy and straw-haired describe man; rather modifies hard, which modifies mouth; supercilious modifies manner. Each addition narrows the picture.
Her face was sad and lovely with bright things in it, bright eyes and a bright passionate mouth
Appositive phrase: bright eyes and a bright passionate mouth names the bright things. The sentence names the category first and then the items.

Modifiers and appositives pack information into one sentence. A short sentence reports. A sentence with an appositive and two stacked adjectives creates a portrait.

Practice. Write one sentence about a room, a street, or a person using one appositive and two modifiers. Underline the appositive and circle the words it renames.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Six words of wealth and class from Chapter 1.

prominent (adjective) · well known and important in a community
Root: Latin prominere, to jut out
In the text: My family have been prominent, well-to-do people in this Middle Western city for three generations.
supercilious (adjective) · looking down on others with quiet contempt
Root: Latin supercilium, eyebrow, from the raised brow of scorn
In the text: Now he was a sturdy straw-haired man of thirty, with a rather hard mouth and a supercilious manner.
arrogant (adjective) · overly proud and sure of one’s own importance
Root: Latin arrogare, to claim for oneself
In the text: Two shining arrogant eyes had established dominance over his face and gave him the appearance of always leaning aggressively forward.
complacency (noun) · calm satisfaction with oneself that is not examined
Root: Latin complacere, to please greatly
In the text: There was something pathetic in his concentration, as if his complacency, more acute than of old, was not enough to him any more.
superficial (adjective) · concerned with the surface only
Root: Latin superficies, surface
In the text: I lived at West Egg, the—well, the less fashionable of the two, though this is a most superficial tag
sinister (adjective) · suggesting that something harmful lies beneath
Root: Latin sinister, on the left, later ominous
In the text: the bizarre and not a little sinister contrast between them
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
nouveau riche (French) · literally “new rich”; people who have recently acquired wealth, often said with mild scorn for showy spending
Example: The mansions of West Egg looked nouveau riche to the families across the bay.
