📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A final edit works in passes, one concern at a time. The Works Cited list follows fixed rules.
Editing for structure, sentences, conventions, and citations in separate passes catches more than one general read. MLA format makes the list of sources consistent and checkable.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can edit a draft in separate passes for structure, sentences, conventions, and citations.
2
I can build a Works Cited list in MLA 9 format and order it correctly.
3
I can use this week’s Word Study words accurately.
📚 PART 1 · EDITING IN PASSES

The strongest edits come from reading the draft several times with a different question each time. One pass for everything misses most errors.

PassQuestionTypical fixes
StructureDoes each section do the job named in its subhead?Move, merge, or cut a section; add the missing counterargument.
SentencesCan each sentence lose words without losing meaning?Cut filler, repair parallel structure, and replace vague verbs.
ConventionsAre punctuation, tense, spelling, and names correct?Fix commas, tense shifts, and quotation punctuation.
CitationsDoes every quotation and borrowed idea have a citation, and does every citation have an entry?Add missing citations; match each one to the Works Cited list.
✎ ON THE BOARD · BEFORE AND AFTER
There is a part where Gatsby’s father shows Nick a book that has a schedule in it that Gatsby wrote when he was a boy.
Wordy: filler phrases and a repeated "that."
Mr. Gatz shows Nick a boyhood schedule written on the flyleaf of a book.
Edited: the subject and verb come first, and each detail earns its place.
Nick calls Tom and Daisy “careless people” (Fitzgerald, ch. 9).
Quotation, then the citation, then the period of the sentence.

Two habits help. Read the draft aloud, because the ear catches clumsy sentences the eye skips. Then read the sentences in reverse order, last to first, to check each one without the pull of the argument.

📚 PART 2 · MLA 9 WORKS CITED

MLA 9 gives every source the same pattern. The core elements appear in this order, each followed by a period or comma, and any element that does not apply is skipped.

Author. Title of source. Title of container, Other contributors, Version, Number, Publisher, Publication date, Location.
RuleMLA 9 practice
HeadingThe title Works Cited is centered at the top of a new page. It is not bold, italic, underlined, or in quotation marks.
SpacingDouble-space the whole list, with no extra space between entries.
OrderAlphabetical by the first element of each entry, usually the author's last name. With no author, alphabetize by title and ignore A, An, and The.
Hanging indentThe first line of each entry sits at the left margin. Every later line is indented half an inch.
Author namesLast name first: Last, First. Two authors: Last, First, and First Last. Three or more: the first author followed by et al.
TitlesA whole work (book, journal, website) is in italics. A part (article, chapter, web page, poem) is in quotation marks. Use title capitalization.
URLsGive the URL or DOI as the location. Write it without https://, as the course models it, and keep that choice consistent.
MatchEvery source cited in the text appears in the list, and every entry in the list is cited in the text.

Patterns for the sources a feature uses most often. Words in plain type are the parts to fill in.

✎ ON THE BOARD · ENTRY PATTERNS
Fitzgerald, F. Scott. The Great Gatsby. 1925. Project Gutenberg, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.
The novel read as an e-text. Note the hanging indent.
Last, First. “Title of Article.” Title of Journal, vol. 0, no. 0, year, pp. 0–0. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/number.
A journal article read on JSTOR. JSTOR is the second container.
Last, First. “Title of Page.” Title of Website, Publisher if different from the website, day month year, URL.
A web page. Use the date the page was published or last updated.
Last, First. Title of Image. Date made, Archive or Museum, URL.
An image from an archive. Give the creator when known and the repository that holds the item.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words appear in the closing pages of Chapter 9. Learn the sense the novel gives each word.

transitory (adjective) · lasting only a short time
Latin transitorius, from transire, to go across.
In the reading: for a transitory enchanted moment man must have held his breath
aesthetic (adjective) · concerned with beauty and the perception of beauty
Greek aisthetikos, perceptive, from aisthanesthai, to perceive.
In the reading: compelled into an aesthetic contemplation he neither understood nor desired
commensurate (adjective) · equal in measure; matched in size or degree
Latin com-, together + mensurare, to measure.
In the reading: something commensurate to his capacity for wonder
orgiastic (adjective) · wildly excessive; like a frenzied revel
From orgy, Greek orgia, secret rites.
In the reading: the orgiastic future that year by year recedes before us
pander (verb) · to indulge or serve a desire, especially a low one
From Pandarus, the go-between in medieval tellings of the story of Troilus and Cressida.
In the reading: had once pandered in whispers to the last and greatest of all human dreams
obscurity (noun) · the state of being dark, hidden, or unknown
Latin obscurus, covered over, dark.
In the reading: somewhere back in that vast obscurity beyond the city
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
coup de grâce (French)
A finishing blow or stroke that ends something already failing. Literally, a stroke of mercy.
Example: The last unpaid bill delivered the coup de grâce to the failing restaurant.
TAKEAWAY
Edit in passes, one question at a time. In a Works Cited list, order alphabetically, indent every line after the first, and match every entry to a citation in the text.
