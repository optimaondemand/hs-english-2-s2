📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A source is only as useful as the judgment applied to it.
Credibility, bias, and omission are three separate checks. Faulty reasoning can hide inside a source that passes all three.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can find scholarly sources on JSTOR with the school login.
2
I can judge a source for credibility, bias, and omission.
3
I can tell a scholarly source from a popular one and name what each can support.
4
I can identify an incorrect premise, a hasty generalization, and an either-or claim.

📚 PART 2 · THREE CHECKS

Chapter 9 supplies a specimen. After Gatsby’s death, newspaper reports shaped the public story. Nick describes them:

Most of those reports were a nightmare—grotesque, circumstantial, eager, and untrue.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 9 (public domain)

The inquest then fixed the official account:

So Wilson was reduced to a man “deranged by grief” in order that the case might remain in its simplest form.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 9 (public domain)
📝 THREE CHECKS, APPLIED TO THE NEWSPAPER REPORTS
CREDIBILITY
Who is speaking, and how does the speaker know? Someone with a “positive manner,” perhaps a detective, calls Wilson a “madman,” and the reports take their key from his voice.
BIAS
What does the account gain from its version? A single tidy cause serves readers, police, and witnesses who want the matter closed.
OMISSION
What is left out? Myrtle's affair with Tom. Catherine, who might have said anything, says nothing of it, and the case stays in its simplest form.

Credibility concerns the source’s position and method. Bias concerns its interest in the result. Omission concerns what the account does not contain. A source can be sincere and still omit the fact that changes the answer.

📚 PART 3 · SCHOLARLY AND POPULAR

Scholarly and popular sources do different jobs. A peer-reviewed article on Fitzgerald’s use of setting, a magazine feature on the Jazz Age, and a company’s web page about a historic mansion each answer different questions.

CheckScholarly sourcePopular source
AuthorA researcher with named credentials in the field.A staff writer, a columnist, or an unsigned page.
AudienceOther scholars and students of the field.A general reader.
ReviewEvaluated by experts before publication.Edited for style and length; rarely reviewed by experts.
EvidenceFootnotes or a bibliography; claims tied to named sources.Sources mentioned in passing, or not named.
Best useSupport for a claim about interpretation or history.Background, current framing, and leads to better sources.

The popular source is not worthless. It becomes a problem when it is asked to carry a claim that needs expert evidence.

📚 PART 4 · FAULTY REASONING

A source can pass every check above and still reason badly. Three flaws appear often, here with invented sentences about the novel.

FlawWhat it doesExample
Incorrect premiseBuilds the argument on a claim that is false or unproven.“A self-made man owes nothing to anyone, so Gatsby owed Wolfshiem nothing.” The premise is false, and the text supplies Wolfshiem's own claim to the contrary.
Hasty generalizationDraws a broad conclusion from too few cases.“Klipspringer and Wolfshiem stayed away, so people in the East never care about the dead.” Two cases, and the owl-eyed man came.
Either-orOffers two options when more exist.“Gatsby was either a great man or a criminal.” The text presents both, and a third reading is a man who invented himself.
⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND SOURCES
An AI tool can suggest search terms and summarize a topic in plain language. It cannot reliably cite real sources: AI tools regularly produce article titles, authors, and journals that sound convincing but do not exist, or misdescribe a real article. Every source an AI tool names must be found and read on JSTOR itself before it is used.
⚡ TRY IT · JUDGE THE SOURCE
For each case, choose the judgment the evidence supports.
1. A magazine feature on the 1920s quotes a single hotel owner and concludes that all of New York spent the decade at parties. What is the main flaw?
2. A peer-reviewed article on the novel was published decades ago and never mentions the historical event a new argument turns on. What is the best use?
3. An unsigned web page argues that the novel is a warning because it ends in a death, and an unhappy ending proves a book is a warning. What is the weakest link?
KEY TERMS
Credibility: the source’s position and method. Bias: its interest in the result. Omission: what the account leaves out. Peer review: evaluation by experts before publication. Incorrect premise, hasty generalization, either-or: three common flaws in reasoning.
