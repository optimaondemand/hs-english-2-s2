📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Shakespeare’s sentences use older pronouns, older verb endings, and inverted word order.
Each feature is regular once it is named. A reader who can find the subject and the verb in an inverted line can follow almost any line in the play.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify thou, thee, thy, and thine and give the modern equivalent.
2
I can read -est and -eth verb endings and name the subject each one goes with.
3
I can restore an inverted line to plain order by finding its subject and verb.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · PRONOUNS AND ENDINGS

Early Modern English had separate forms for addressing one person (thou, thee, thy) and for addressing several or showing respect (you, your). Verbs changed their endings to match.

Pronoun key
thou = you (subject)
thee = you (object)
thy, thine = your, yours
-est ending: goes with thou (lovest, hast)
-eth or -th ending: goes with he, she, it (hath, loveth)

Inverted order. English normally puts the subject before the verb. Verse often reverses that order or moves a phrase to the front, to fit the meter or to stress a word. To read an inverted line, find the verb first, ask who or what does it, and then rebuild the sentence in plain order.

✎ ON THE BOARD · THREE KINDS OF CHANGE
Hippolyta, I woo’d thee with my sword, / And won thy love doing thee injuries;
thee is the object form of thou (you as object); thy means your.
This man hath bewitch’d the bosom of my child.
hath = has. The old ending -th goes with he, she, it.
If thou lovest me then, / Steal forth thy father’s house tomorrow night;
thou is the subject, so the verb takes -est: lovest = love.
Full of vexation come I, with complaint / Against my child, my daughter Hermia.
Inverted order. Plain order: I come full of vexation, with complaint against my child. Subject: I. Verb: come.
With cunning hast thou filch’d my daughter’s heart,
Inverted order. Plain order: Thou hast filched my daughter’s heart with cunning. Subject: thou. Verb: hast filched.
⚡ TRY IT · SUBJECT, VERB, AND FORM
For each line, choose the reading that fits the grammar.
1. “With cunning hast thou filch’d my daughter’s heart.” What are the subject and the verb?
2. “Rather your eyes must with his judgment look.” What are the subject and the verb?
3. “What’s the news with thee?” What does thee do in the sentence?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the first scene, where law and authority are the subject. Each entry gives the sense the play uses.

privilege (noun) · a right held by one person or group by law or custom
Root: Latin privilegium, a law for one person, from privus (private) + lex (law)
I beg the ancient privilege of Athens:
extenuate (verb) · to lessen the force of something; to soften
Root: Latin extenuare, to make thin, from ex- + tenuis (thin)
Which by no means we may extenuate
abjure (verb) · to swear off; to renounce on oath
Root: Latin abjurare, to deny on oath, from ab- (away) + jurare (swear)
Either to die the death, or to abjure / For ever the society of men.
edict (noun) · an order issued by authority; a decree
Root: Latin edictum, a proclamation, from edicere (to declare)
It stands as an edict in destiny.
sovereignty (noun) · supreme authority over a person or a realm
Root: Old French soverain, from Latin super (above)
My soul consents not to give sovereignty.
consent (noun) · agreement given freely
Root: Latin consentire, to feel together, from com- (together) + sentire (feel)
This man hath my consent to marry her.
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

Modern editions of plays begin with a list of characters. English kept the Latin phrase for it, and it also describes any cast of people involved in a situation.

dramatis personae (Latin: “persons of the drama”)
A modern edition of the play opens with the dramatis personae, so a reader can tell Egeus from Theseus before the first scene begins.
🔑 KEY TERMS
inverted order: a sentence with the subject after the verb or a phrase moved to the front.
subject: the word that does the action or is described.
verb: the word or words that name the action or state.
object: the word that receives the action or follows a preposition.
