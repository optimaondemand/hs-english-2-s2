The New Colossus
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A sonnet written to help pay for a pedestal gives the statue a voice and a promise. The novel tests the promise.
Emma Lazarus wrote “The New Colossus” in 1883 for a fundraising effort for the pedestal of the Statue of Liberty, a gift from France designed by Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi and dedicated in 1886. The poem is short, strict in form, and one of the best-known statements of the American promise to newcomers.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify the sonnet’s rhyme scheme, its two parts, and its turn.
2
I can explain the contrast between the “brazen giant” and the new statue.
3
I can compare the poem’s promise with the novel’s images of arrival and aspiration.
📋 READING
Read the poem below, aloud if possible.
📚 PART 1 · THE POEM
Not like the brazen giant of Greek fame,
With conquering limbs astride from land to land;
Here at our sea-washed, sunset gates shall stand
A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame
Is the imprisoned lightning, and her name
Mother of Exiles. From her beacon-hand
Glows world-wide welcome; her mild eyes command
The air-bridged harbor that twin cities frame.
“Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!” cries she
With silent lips. “Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!”
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)
📚 PART 2 · FORM

A sonnet has fourteen lines. This one follows the Italian pattern: an eight-line octave that rhymes ABBAABBA and a six-line sestet that rhymes CDCDCD. The volta, or turn, falls between them. Here the turn is also a change of speaker: the octave describes the statue, and the sestet lets her speak.

📝 THE SHAPE OF THE SONNET · END WORDS BY RHYME
OCTAVE · LINES 1 TO 8 · A B B A A B B A
Describes the statue and defines her against the old giant.
A fameB landB standA flameA nameB handB commandA frame
THE TURN: the statue stops being described and starts to speak
SESTET · LINES 9 TO 14 · C D C D C D
Gives her words: a refusal of the old lands and an invitation to the poor.

📚 PART 3 · THE OLD GIANT AND THE NEW

The poem opens by rejecting a rival image:

Not like the brazen giant of Greek fame,
With conquering limbs astride from land to land;
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)

The reference is to the Colossus of Rhodes, an ancient bronze statue of the sun god Helios in the harbor of a Greek island. “Brazen” means made of brass, and also shameless. The old giant stands for conquest: in the poem’s image its legs are planted on either side of a harbor, and its power is shown by dominance.

The new statue is set against it in every way:

Here at our sea-washed, sunset gates shall stand
A mighty woman with a torch, whose flame
Is the imprisoned lightning, and her name
Mother of Exiles.
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)

A woman in place of a giant, a torch in place of conquering limbs, welcome in place of conquest. The old giant is mighty by force, and this figure is mighty by receiving. The poem defines the statue by what it refuses to be.

📚 PART 4 · THE SPEECH

The sestet turns the statue into a speaker, and her first words reject the Old World:

“Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!” cries she
With silent lips.
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)

“Storied pomp” is the splendor of old monarchies and old fortunes. The phrase “silent lips” matters too: a statue cannot speak, and the poem lets the reader hear what the statue stands for. Then comes the request, which inverts the usual order of invitations by asking for the unwanted:

Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)

The final image is an act, and not a description: a lamp lifted beside a door. The door is golden, which suggests prosperity, and it is open.

📚 PART 5 · THE PROMISE AND THE NOVEL

Two lights shine across water, one in the poem and one in the novel. One is the lamp of the poem, lifted to welcome. The other is the light at the end of Daisy’s dock, seen from Nick’s lawn:

a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The poem’s light is offered and speaks. The novel’s light is distant and silent, and Gatsby reaches toward it. One is a promise made to the many, and the other is a longing held by one man. Nick makes the point from the other side when he thinks of young men who simply arrive with no history:

But young men didn’t—at least in my provincial inexperience I believed they didn’t—drift coolly out of nowhere and buy a palace on Long Island Sound.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 3 (public domain)

The poem’s America invites the homeless to come in, and the novel’s America asks where a new arrival got his money, and whether the gate that opened for him will stay open. Nick’s world has gates of another kind, and the Buchanans’ dinner table is one:

The idea is if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

Set the three together, and the poem becomes a measuring stick. It states the promise in its purest form, and the novel keeps checking how much of the promise is kept.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE POEM
Choose the best reading for each question.
1. Where does the poem turn from describing the statue to letting her speak?
2. Why does the poem name the “brazen giant of Greek fame” before it describes the new statue?
3. The poem’s lamp and the novel’s green light both shine across water. What separates them?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Sonnet: a fourteen-line poem in a fixed rhyme scheme.
Octave and sestet: the eight-line and six-line parts of an Italian sonnet.
Volta: the point in a sonnet where the thought or the speaker changes.
Foil: a contrasting figure that sharpens the meaning of another.
