What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?
Literature · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Douglass accepts the audience’s document and uses it against the celebration.
On July 5, 1852, Frederick Douglass spoke at Rochester, New York, to an audience gathered around the Fourth of July. The lesson reads excerpts for central idea, purpose, audience, and three kinds of appeal.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state the central idea and the purpose of the speech.
2
I can identify the audience and explain how Douglass addresses it.
3
I can identify ethos, pathos, and logos in specific lines and explain what each does.
4
I can explain how Douglass turns the Declaration back on the people celebrating it.
📋 READING
▶
Read the excerpts quoted below. The full speech is in Gutenberg eBook #202, in the appendix titled “What to the Slave Is the Fourth of July?”
📚 PART 1 · THE OPENING QUESTIONS

The speech begins with questions that challenge the occasion. The audience expects thanks. Douglass asks why he was invited.

Fellow-Citizens—Pardon me, and allow me to ask, why am I called upon to speak here to-day? What have I, or those I represent, to do with your national independence? Are the great principles of political freedom and of natural justice, embodied in that Declaration of Independence, extended to us?
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)

The audience is citizens celebrating independence, and the speaker addresses them as Fellow-Citizens. The reference to that Declaration of Independence sets the standard for the whole speech: the principles the audience claims.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
A speaker is invited to a celebration whose promises do not reach the speaker’s community. Which opening is most likely to hold the audience?
📚 PART 2 · THE CENTRAL IDEA

The central idea follows. The blessings of the day are not shared, and the speaker is not included in them.

I am not included within the pale of this glorious anniversary! Your high independence only reveals the immeasurable distance between us. The blessings in which you this day rejoice, are not enjoyed in common. The rich inheritance of justice, liberty, prosperity, and independence, bequeathed by your fathers, is shared by you, not by me. The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me. This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. You may rejoice, I must mourn.
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)

Two paired short sentences carry the idea: This Fourth of July is yours, not mine. and You may rejoice, I must mourn. The parallel form sets the audience and the speaker on opposite sides of one day. The central idea is that a celebration of liberty reveals the gap between the nation’s principle and its practice.

📚 PART 3 · ETHOS, PATHOS, AND LOGOS

Douglass uses all three appeals. The audience’s own document supplies the strongest reasoning.

Would you have me argue that man is entitled to liberty? that he is the rightful owner of his own body? You have already declared it. Must I argue the wrongfulness of slavery? Is that a question for republicans?
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)
The manhood of the slave is conceded. It is admitted in the fact that southern statute books are covered with enactments forbidding, under severe fines and penalties, the teaching of the slave to read or write.
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)
📝 THE THREE APPEALS IN THE SPEECH
ETHOS
He establishes honest motive and plain speech: I will not equivocate; I will not excuse;. He says he would gladly join the celebration if the principles were extended to him.
PATHOS
He sets the audience’s life against his own: The sunlight that brought life and healing to you, has brought stripes and death to me.
LOGOS
He argues from premises the audience accepts. The Declaration says liberty is a right: You have already declared it. The slave codes, he says, show that the nation already treats the enslaved as moral beings.

The reasoning has two steps. The audience affirms that liberty is a natural right. The audience also passes laws, in Douglass’s account, that punish the enslaved as responsible persons and forbid teaching them to read. The nation’s practice contradicts its principle, and the contradiction comes from the nation’s own words.

📚 PART 4 · PURPOSE: FIRE, NOT LIGHT

Douglass states his purpose in the contrast between light and fire.

For it is not light that is needed, but fire; it is not the gentle shower, but thunder. We need the storm, the whirlwind, and the earthquake.
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)

A speaker who wants to supply information offers light. Douglass says information is not what the audience lacks. He wants to rouse a conscience, and the pairing of “light” with “fire” and “gentle shower” with “thunder” names the change from persuasion to alarm. He then answers his own question.

What to the American slave is your Fourth of July? I answer, a day that reveals to him, more than all other days in the year, the gross injustice and cruelty to which he is the constant victim.
Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom, appendix, speech of July 5, 1852 (public domain)

In the Declaration (read earlier in the module), the writers argue from principle to grievance to separation. Douglass keeps the principle and turns the charge around. He begins from the principle the audience affirms and asks whether the nation itself has applied it.

🔑 KEY TERMS
Central idea
The main point of the speech, stated in one sentence.
Purpose
What the speaker intends the audience to think, feel, or do.
Audience
The people addressed; here, citizens at an Independence celebration.
Ethos, pathos, logos
Appeals to the speaker’s credibility, to emotion, and to reason.
