Integrating and Punctuating Quotations
Grammar + Vocab · Week 6
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A quotation works best when it is part of the writer’s own sentence and punctuated so the reader can tell whose words are whose.
Signal phrases, brackets, and ellipses let a writer fit another author’s words into a sentence without changing them. The punctuation follows fixed rules.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can introduce a quotation with a signal phrase, a that-clause, or a colon.
2
I can use brackets and an ellipsis to change or shorten a quotation honestly.
3
I can place periods, commas, question marks, and citations correctly around a quotation.
4
I can use this week’s Word Study words accurately.
📚 PART 1 · INTEGRATING AND PUNCTUATING

Four patterns cover most uses. Each example quotes a line from this week’s reading.

✎ ON THE BOARD · FOUR WAYS TO INTEGRATE
Nick admits, “I disapproved of him from beginning to end.”
Signal phrase, then a comma, then the quotation as a full sentence. The period stays inside the quotation marks.
Wilson insists that “God sees everything.”
A that-clause makes the quotation part of the writer’s grammar. No comma follows “that.”
Nick judges Gatsby's end in one phrase: “paid a high price for living too long with a single dream.”
A colon follows a complete clause and introduces the quotation.
Nick says “[Gatsby] was a captain before he went to the front.”
Brackets replace a word, here a pronoun, so the quotation makes sense in the new sentence.

An ellipsis shows that words were omitted. The omitted words must not change the meaning.

✎ ON THE BOARD · OMISSION AND QUESTION MARKS
Lincoln resolves “that these dead shall not have died in vain ... that this nation, under God, shall have a new birth of freedom.”
An ellipsis marks the omitted words between the two clauses. MLA style spaces the three dots (. . .).
Why does Nick tell Gatsby that he is worth “the whole damn bunch put together”?
The question mark belongs to the writer’s sentence, so it falls outside the quotation marks. If the quoted words were themselves a question, it would fall inside.
Michaelis answers, “That’s an advertisement.”
The period of a quoted sentence stays inside the marks. A citation, if required, comes after the closing marks and before the final period.

Three errors are common. A dropped quotation stands alone with no introduction. A comma after “that” splits the quotation from its own sentence. Changed words that are not in brackets misquote the source.

⚡ TRY IT · PUNCTUATE THE QUOTATION
Choose the correct sentence.
1. Which sentence integrates the quotation correctly?
2. Where does the question mark go? Why does Nick say Gatsby is worth the whole damn bunch put together?
3. Which version replaces a pronoun with a name correctly? The original reads “He was a captain before he went to the front.”
📚 WORD STUDY

Six words from this week’s reading. Each helps in weighing sources and claims.

proposition (noun) · a claim put forward to be tested or proved
Root: Latin proponere, “to set forth”
In the text: dedicated to the proposition that all men are created equal (Gettysburg Address)
corroborate (verb) · to confirm with independent evidence
Root: Latin corroborare, “to strengthen”: con-, “together,” + robur, “strength”
In the text: as if he hoped I’d corroborate this (Chapter 8)
incoherent (adjective) · not logically connected; hard to follow
Root: Latin in-, “not,” + cohaerere, “to stick together”
In the text: the quality of Wilson’s incoherent muttering changed (Chapter 8)
garrulous (adjective) · talkative, especially about unimportant matters
Root: Latin garrulus, from garrire, “to chatter”
In the text: some garrulous man telling over and over what had happened (Chapter 8)
interminable (adjective) · seeming to have no end
Root: Latin in-, “not,” + terminare, “to end”
In the text: an interminable amount of stock (Chapter 8)
consecrate (verb) · to make sacred; to set apart for a high purpose
Root: Latin consecrare: con-, “with,” + sacer, “sacred”
In the text: we can not dedicate—we can not consecrate—we can not hallow—this ground (Gettysburg Address)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN
caveat (Latin, “let him beware”)
Meaning: a warning or a stated condition
In a sentence: The report is useful, with one caveat: it covers only one town.
