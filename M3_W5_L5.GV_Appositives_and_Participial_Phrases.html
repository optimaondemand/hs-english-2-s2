📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Detail can ride inside a sentence instead of in a new one.
An appositive renames a noun, and a participial phrase describes one. Act 5 uses both to introduce the players and to describe their actions in a few words.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify appositives and participial phrases in a sentence.
2
I can combine two short sentences with an appositive or a participial phrase.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · APPOSITIVES AND PARTICIPIAL PHRASES

An appositive is a noun or noun phrase placed beside another noun to rename it. A participial phrase is built on a verb form ending in -ing or -ed, and it describes a noun. Both add detail without a new sentence. Nonessential appositives and participial phrases are set off with commas. A participial phrase should sit next to the noun it describes.

✎ ON THE BOARD · APPOSITIVES AND PARTICIPIAL PHRASES IN ACT 5
Snug: “I, one Snug the joiner, am”
Appositive. one Snug the joiner renames I and sits between commas.
Prologue: “Anon comes Pyramus, sweet youth, and tall,”
Appositive. sweet youth, and tall renames Pyramus and adds two qualities in one phrase.
Prologue: “And Thisbe, tarrying in mulberry shade,”
Participial phrase. tarrying in mulberry shade describes Thisbe and is set off by commas.
Hippolyta: “And all their minds transfigur’d so together,”
Participial phrase with a past participle. transfigur'd so together describes minds.
Hippolyta: “And duty in his service perishing.”
Participial phrase. in his service perishing follows duty and describes it.
Flat: Snout is a tinker. Snout plays Wall.
Two short sentences with a repeated subject.
Joined: Snout, a tinker, plays Wall.
An appositive removes the repetition and keeps the commas.
Joined: Holding up his fingers, Wall makes the chink.
A participial phrase at the start, followed by a comma, describing Wall.

Misplacement changes meaning. Running from the lion, Thisbe’s mantle fell suggests the mantle ran. Running from the lion, Thisbe dropped her mantle puts the phrase next to the noun that did the running.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

The six words below come from Act 5, Scene 1. The theme is appearance and reality.

apprehend (verb) to take hold of; to grasp as true
Root: Latin apprehendere: ad-, “toward,” plus prehendere, “to seize.”
In the text: “Such shaping fantasies, that apprehend” (Theseus)
compact (adjective) composed of; made up of
Root: Latin compactus, “put together,” from compingere. The same root gives compact, “small and dense”; compact, “an agreement,” is a different word.
In the text: “Are of imagination all compact:” (Theseus)
transfigured (verb, past participle) changed in form or appearance
Root: Latin transfigurare: trans-, “across,” plus figura, “form.”
In the text: “And all their minds transfigur’d so together,” (Hippolyta)
antique (adjective) ancient and improbable; of old, unbelievable stories
Root: Latin antiquus, “old, former.”
In the text: “These antique fables, nor these fairy toys.” (Theseus)
palpable (adjective) obvious; able to be felt
Root: Late Latin palpabilis, from palpare, “to touch gently.”
In the text: “This palpable-gross play hath well beguil’d” (Theseus)
beguile (verb) to pass time pleasantly; also to deceive or charm
Root: Middle English: be- plus guile, from Old French guile, “trick.” The two senses mirror the appearance-and-reality theme.
In the text: “How shall we beguile / The lazy time, if not with some delight?” (Theseus)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
faux pas (from French faux pas)
Meaning: a social blunder; literally “false step,” from French faux, “false,” and pas, “step.”
Example: “Talking over the actors would be a faux pas in a theater today; the court in Act 5 does it freely.”
