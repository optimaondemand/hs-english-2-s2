📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Transitions show how one idea leads to the next. Elaboration gives an idea enough room to land.
A paragraph of correct sentences can still read as a list. Connecting words and added detail turn a list into reasoning.

📚 PART 1 · TRANSITIONS

A transition names the relationship between two ideas. The reader should never have to guess whether the next sentence adds, contrasts, explains, or follows. Fitzgerald’s narrator signals these relationships constantly.

✎ ON THE BOARD · TRANSITIONS IN CHAPTER 9
After that I felt a certain shame for Gatsby
After that marks sequence: one event follows another.
However, that was my fault, for he was one of those who used to sneer most bitterly at Gatsby
However reverses direction. The sentence first blames the caller, then turns the blame on Nick himself.
So Wilson was reduced to a man “deranged by grief” in order that the case might remain in its simplest form.
So marks result: the official account follows from what was said before.
But all this part of it seemed remote and unessential.
But turns from the news reports to Nick's own concern.
RelationshipTransitionsUse
Additionalso, moreover, in additionA second point of the same kind.
Contrastbut, however, yet, in contrastA turn against what was just said.
Cause and resultso, therefore, as a result, consequentlyThe second idea follows from the first.
Examplefor example, for instance, in particularA case that makes a claim concrete.
Sequenceafter that, then, finally, meanwhileEvents or steps in order.

Transitions work between sentences and between paragraphs. A transition that names the wrong relationship misleads: therefore after an unrelated fact claims a logic that does not exist.

📚 PART 2 · ELABORATION

Elaboration develops a claim after it is stated. A reliable pattern has three moves: state the idea, give a specific example or detail, then explain what the example proves.

✎ ON THE BOARD · THIN AND ELABORATED
Klipspringer calls about his shoes. He does not come to the funeral. He is a guest from the parties.
Thin: three true statements with no connection and no point.
Klipspringer, a guest who stayed in Gatsby’s house for weeks, telephones only to ask for his shoes. For example, he gives a picnic as his reason for missing the funeral, and he asks about tennis shoes in the same call. This shows that the house mattered to him and its owner did not.
Elaborated: a detail, an example, and an explanation of what the example shows.

The explanation is the move most often skipped. An example without an explanation leaves the reader to draw the conclusion, and the reader may draw a different one.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words appear in Chapter 9, in Nick’s account of the newspapers and the inquest, and in his judgments of himself and his home. Learn the sense the novel gives each word.

adventitious (adjective) · coming from outside; not part of a thing’s real nature
Latin adventicius, from advenire, to arrive (ad-, to + venire, to come).
In the reading: the adventitious authority of his voice set the key for the newspaper reports
pasquinade (noun) · a short satire or lampoon, often published without the author’s name
French, from Italian Pasquino, the name of a statue in Rome on which satirical verses were posted.
In the reading: served up in racy pasquinade
superfluous (adjective) · more than is needed; unnecessary
Latin superfluus, overflowing (super-, over + fluere, to flow).
In the reading: That request seemed superfluous when I wrote it.
inquest (noun) · an official inquiry into the cause of a death
Old French enqueste, from Latin inquirere, to seek into (in-, into + quaerere, to seek).
In the reading: Michaelis’s testimony at the inquest brought to light Wilson’s suspicions of his wife
complacent (adjective) · satisfied with oneself, often without good reason
Latin complacere, to please greatly (com- + placere, to please).
In the reading: a little complacent from growing up in the Carraway house
provincial (adjective) · narrow in outlook; marked by the limits of a small place
Latin provincialis, from provincia, a territory under Roman rule.
In the reading: rid of my provincial squeamishness forever
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
ennui (French)
A feeling of weary dissatisfaction, often from having nothing that engages the mind or spirit.
Example: The ennui of long afternoons at the empty mansion settled over the guests once the music stopped.
TAKEAWAY
A transition names the relationship between two ideas. Elaboration adds a detail and explains what it shows. A paragraph needs both.
