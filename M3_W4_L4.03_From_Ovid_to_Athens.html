📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The mechanicals’ play is Ovid’s story with a different voice.
Pyramus and Thisbe first appears in Latin, in Ovid’s Metamorphoses. Shakespeare keeps the plot almost intact and changes the register, the stage business, and the ending. The comparison shows what adaptation can keep, change, add, and cut.

2

3

📚 PART 1 · THE STORY IN OVID

Ovid tells the story of Pyramus and Thisbe in Book IV of the Metamorphoses, a collection of Latin tales of transformation. In Riley’s prose translation, the lovers live in neighboring houses and are forbidden to marry:

They would have united themselves, too, by the tie of marriage, but their fathers forbade it.
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)

The wall between the houses has a flaw, and the lovers use it to talk:

The party-wall, common to the two houses, was cleft by a small chink, which it had got formerly, when it was built.
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)

They plan to escape the city at night and meet at a tomb. A lioness frightens Thisbe away, and she drops her veil:

Lo! a lioness approaches, having her foaming jaws besmeared with the recent slaughter of oxen
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)
and, while she flies, her veil falling from her back, she leaves it behind.
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)

Pyramus arrives and finds the bloodied veil. He blames himself:

One night will be the ruin of two lovers, of whom she was the most deserving of a long life. My soul is guilty; ’tis I that have destroyed thee, much to be lamented;
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)

Both die. Thisbe’s last request is that the mulberry tree keep the memory, and the story ends with the gods and the parents granting it:

ever bear fruit black and suited for mourning, as a memorial of the blood of us two.
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)
what was left of them, from the funeral pile, reposed in the same urn.
Ovid, Metamorphoses, Book IV, trans. H. T. Riley (1851, public domain)
📚 PART 2 · WHAT THE MECHANICALS KEEP

Quince’s company performs the same story in Act 5. Nearly every element of Ovid’s plot is present. The company has to stage them with workmen’s resources, so each element becomes a person or a prop. The wall is Snout, who explains himself: “And such a wall as I would have you think / That had in it a crannied hole or chink,” The meeting place stays the tomb, though Pyramus calls it “Ninny’s tomb”. The prologue keeps the mulberry: “And Thisbe, tarrying in mulberry shade,” The lion tears the mantle, and Pyramus finds it and kills himself under a mistake. The structure is Ovid’s: a prohibition, a flawed wall, a night meeting, a misread sign, two deaths.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT THEY CHANGE

The changes are mostly changes of register. Ovid’s lioness is a real danger. The company’s lion stops to reassure the ladies: “Then know that I, one Snug the joiner, am / A lion fell, nor else no lion’s dam;” Ovid’s Pyramus blames himself. The company’s Pyramus blames Nature and then repeats a word: “O wherefore, Nature, didst thou lions frame,” and later “Thus die I, thus, thus, thus.” The lovers whisper through the chink with real longing in Ovid. In the company’s version Thisbe says “I kiss the wall’s hole, not your lips at all.” The names of famous lovers are garbled (Leander becomes Limander, Cephalus and Procris become Shafalus and Procrus). And the ending changes the stakes: in Ovid the gods and parents are moved, and in the court Bottom says only “No, I assure you; the wall is down that parted their fathers.” The tragedy is told by people who cannot tell it, and the story becomes funny without ceasing to be sad.

The story also mirrors the frame. Lysander and Hermia face a father’s prohibition and plan a night flight to meet outside the city. Lysander calls the flight an escape “Without the peril of the Athenian law.” The play inside the play gives the audience a tragic version of the situation the lovers have just left, performed for the court that ruled on it.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE CHANGE DO?
For each item, choose the reading the evidence supports.
1. Ovid has a lioness. Shakespeare has Snug, who says he is “one Snug the joiner.” What does the change do?
2. Ovid’s Pyramus says “My soul is guilty; ’tis I that have destroyed thee.” The company’s Pyramus says “O wherefore, Nature, didst thou lions frame.” Which comparison is supported?
💪 TAKEAWAY

An adaptation can keep a plot and change its meaning. The mechanicals keep the wall, the tomb, the beast, and the mistaken sign, and the changes of voice and ending turn a tragedy into a comedy that still has a tragic shape.
