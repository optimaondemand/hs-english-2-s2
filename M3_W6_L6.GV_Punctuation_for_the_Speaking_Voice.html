📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Commas, colons, semicolons, and dashes are marks of breath.
In verse, punctuation guides the speaking voice as much as the grammar. Each mark asks for a different kind of pause, and a line that ends without one carries the voice across the break.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain what a comma, semicolon, colon, period, and dash ask of a speaker.
2
I can read a passage of verse aloud, pausing at each mark.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · PUNCTUATION AS PAUSE

Punctuation in verse works as a guide to breath and pause. A comma asks for a short pause. A semicolon asks for a longer one, between two complete thoughts. A colon points forward to what comes next. A period is a full stop. A dash marks a break: an interruption, a turn, or a correction. A line that ends with no mark carries the voice across the line break. The marks in a modern edition are the editors’, so two editions may pause in different places.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PUNCTUATION AS PAUSE IN ACT 5
Puck: “If we shadows have offended, / Think but this, and all is mended,”
Commas close each short phrase. Read each as a small breath before the next piece of the plea.
Theseus: “Lovers, to bed; ’tis almost fairy time.”
The comma after Lovers addresses them. The semicolon separates the order from its reason with a longer pause.
Theseus: “That is the madman: the lover, all as frantic,”
The colon points ahead. The voice lifts slightly and the next phrase explains the last.
Pyramus: “Which is—no, no—which was the fairest dame”
Dashes mark a break of thought. He stops, corrects himself, and resumes.
Puck: “That you have but slumber’d here / While these visions did appear.”
No mark at the end of the first line. The voice carries across the break and rests at the period.

Read the five passages aloud and pause at each mark. Then read them again with no pauses, and compare how the meaning changes.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

The six words below come from the close of Act 5, Scene 1. The theme is performance words.

ditty (noun) a short, simple song
Root: Old French dite, from Latin dictatum, “something dictated.”
In the text: “And this ditty after me,” (Oberon)
rehearse (verb) to practice a piece before performing it
Root: Old French rehercier, “to go over again,” from re- plus hercer, “to harrow.”
In the text: “First rehearse your song by rote,” (Titania)
rote (noun) memory by repetition, without attention to meaning
Root: Middle English rote, origin uncertain. By rote means from memory.
In the text: “First rehearse your song by rote,” (Titania)
reprehend (verb) to find fault with; to blame
Root: Latin reprehendere: re-, “back,” plus prehendere, “to seize.”
In the text: “Gentles, do not reprehend.” (Puck)
amends (noun) something given to make up for a wrong
Root: Old French amendes, from amender, “to better.” Related to mend.
In the text: “And Robin shall restore amends.” (Puck)
consecrate (verb (here a participle)) to make holy; to set apart
Root: Latin consecrare: con- plus sacrare, from sacer, “sacred.”
In the text: “With this field-dew consecrate,” (Oberon)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
encore (from French encore)
Meaning: again, still. In English, a call from an audience for a performance to be repeated, or the extra piece a performer gives in answer.
Example: “After the final bow, the audience called for an encore.”
