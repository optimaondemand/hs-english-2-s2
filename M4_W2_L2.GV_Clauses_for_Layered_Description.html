📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A clause adds a layer to a noun. Stacking clauses builds a description in depth.
This lesson covers adjective clauses (who, which, where, that) and adverb clauses (when, where, as, though) in sentences from Chapters 1 to 3, with Word Study on words of illusion and display.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify an adjective clause and the noun it describes.
2
I can tell a restrictive clause from a nonrestrictive one by its commas.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · CLAUSES THAT DESCRIBE

A clause has a subject and a verb. An adjective clause describes a noun and begins with who, whom, which, that, or where. An adverb clause tells when, where, why, or how something happens and begins with a word such as when, as, because, or though. A nonrestrictive clause adds extra information and is set off by commas. A restrictive clause identifies which one is meant and takes no commas.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CLAUSES IN THE NOVEL
This is a valley of ashes—a fantastic farm where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens
Adjective clause: where ashes grow like wheat into ridges and hills and grotesque gardens. It describes the farm and turns a place into a picture.
stir up an impenetrable cloud, which screens their obscure operations from your sight
Nonrestrictive adjective clause: which screens their obscure operations from your sight. It adds a fact about the cloud, and a comma sets it off.
Tom Buchanan, who had been hovering restlessly about the room, stopped and rested his hand on my shoulder.
Nonrestrictive adjective clause: who had been hovering restlessly about the room. It describes Tom Buchanan, who is already identified.
Once there they were introduced by somebody who knew Gatsby
Restrictive adjective clause: who knew Gatsby. It identifies which somebody, so no comma is used.
When I came back from the East last autumn I felt that I wanted the world to be in uniform and at a sort of moral attention forever
Adverb clause: When I came back from the East last autumn. It tells when the feeling came.

The test for restrictive and nonrestrictive is to remove the clause. If the sentence still identifies the same person or thing, the clause was extra and takes commas. If the meaning changes, the clause was needed and takes none.

Practice. Write a sentence describing a place, using one clause that begins with where or which. Decide whether the clause is restrictive or nonrestrictive, and punctuate it to match.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

Six words of illusion and display from Chapters 2 and 3.

grotesque (adjective) · strangely distorted, fantastic, or ugly
Root: Italian grottesco, from grotta, cave, for the odd decorations found in buried Roman rooms
In the text: ridges and hills and grotesque gardens
transcendent (adjective) · going beyond ordinary limits
Root: Latin transcendere, to climb over
In the text: with a transcendent effort, of ash-grey men
impenetrable (adjective) · impossible to pass through or see through
Root: Latin in-, not, + penetrare, to enter
In the text: stir up an impenetrable cloud
sumptuous (adjective) · splendid and obviously expensive
Root: Latin sumptus, expense
In the text: sumptuous and romantic apartments were concealed overhead
prodigality (noun) · reckless, lavish spending or giving
Root: Latin prodigus, wasteful
In the text: Laughter is easier minute by minute, spilled with prodigality, tipped out at a cheerful word.
vacuous (adjective) · empty, without thought or content
Root: Latin vacuus, empty
In the text: while happy, vacuous bursts of laughter rose toward the summer sky
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
chauffeur (French) · originally a stoker who fed the fire of an early steam car; now a person employed to drive a private car
Example: A chauffeur in a uniform of robin’s-egg blue crossed the lawn with the invitation.
