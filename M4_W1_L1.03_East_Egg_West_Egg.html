East Egg, West Egg
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Two peninsulas, identical in shape, divided by money. The setting does the work of a social map.
Fitzgerald places the novel on Long Island, twenty miles from New York. Chapter 1 sets two communities side by side and lets their differences explain the people who live in them.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe the contrast between East Egg and West Egg using the text’s details.
2
I can explain how a house can signal its owner’s position and history.
3
I can identify the first appearance of the green light and what the narrator can and cannot see.
📋 READING
Reread the descriptions of the two Eggs, the Buchanans’ house, and the last paragraph of Chapter 1 as needed.
📚 PART 1 · TWO EGGS

Nick’s opening survey of the geography is a joke with a point:

Twenty miles from the city a pair of enormous eggs, identical in contour and separated only by a courtesy bay, jut out into the most domesticated body of salt water in the Western hemisphere, the great wet barnyard of Long Island Sound.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)
To the wingless a more interesting phenomenon is their dissimilarity in every particular except shape and size.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The shapes match, and everything else differs. Nick lives on the less fashionable side and says the label is incomplete:

I lived at West Egg, the—well, the less fashionable of the two, though this is a most superficial tag to express the bizarre and not a little sinister contrast between them.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The word “superficial” is Nick’s own warning: the difference between the Eggs is deeper than fashion. The novel keeps returning to what the difference means.

📚 PART 2 · WEST EGG: A HOUSE AS A STATEMENT
📝 THE GEOGRAPHY OF CHAPTER 1 (SCHEMATIC, NOT TO SCALE)
WEST EGG
Less fashionable. Nick’s small house at the tip, squeezed between two large places. Gatsby’s mansion on Nick’s right.
courtesy bay
a light on a dock
EAST EGG
Fashionable. white palaces along the water. The Buchanans’ red-and-white mansion overlooking the bay.
Long Island Sound to the north. New York City twenty miles to the west.

Gatsby’s house is the first large object Nick describes:

it was a factual imitation of some Hôtel de Ville in Normandy, with a tower on one side, spanking new under a thin beard of raw ivy, and a marble swimming pool, and more than forty acres of lawn and garden.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

Each phrase carries a message. A town hall from Normandy copied for a private home signals wealth that borrows its prestige from Europe. “Spanking new” and “raw ivy” mean the money is recent: real old houses have grown their ivy. The house is large and handsome and has no past.

📚 PART 3 · EAST EGG: THE BUCHANANS

Across the bay, Nick finds the opposite style:

Their house was even more elaborate than I expected, a cheerful red-and-white Georgian Colonial mansion, overlooking the bay. The lawn started at the beach and ran towards the front door for a quarter of a mile, jumping over sundials and brick walks and burning gardens
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

The Georgian Colonial style recalls an American past with an address attached. Tom’s own remark about the house is instructive:

“I’ve got a nice place here,” he said, his eyes flashing about restlessly.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)
“It belonged to Demaine, the oil man.”
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

Tom points out the property and credits it to a previous owner in the same breath. The Buchanans are wealthy and mobile, and they do not belong to the place as deeply as their manner suggests. The two Eggs differ in age of money and in confidence more than in wealth.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Which detail of a house says the most about its owners?

The women of the house are introduced in the same visual register:

They were both in white, and their dresses were rippling and fluttering as if they had just been blown back in after a short flight around the house.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

White, light, and weightless: the picture is of people who are carried by money and do not touch the ground.

📚 PART 4 · THE LIGHT ACROSS THE WATER

The chapter ends at night on Nick’s lawn, with a view in two directions. He sees his neighbor standing at the water’s edge:

he stretched out his arms toward the dark water in a curious way, and, far as I was from him, I could have sworn he was trembling. Involuntarily I glanced seaward—and distinguished nothing except a single green light, minute and far away, that might have been the end of a dock.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

Nick reports only what he can see. He sees a man, a gesture, and a small light. He does not know what the light means, and the careful phrase “might have been” marks the limit of his knowledge. The first view of Gatsby is a man reaching for something across the water that cannot be reached by walking across the lawn. The bay that separates the two Eggs is the distance the novel will ask the reader to measure.

⚡ TRY IT · READING THE SETTING
Choose the best reading for each detail.
1. Gatsby’s house is a “factual imitation” of a Normandy town hall, “spanking new” under “raw ivy”. What does this suggest?
2. Nick “could have sworn” Gatsby was trembling and sees only a light that “might have been” a dock. Why does Fitzgerald limit the view?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Setting: the place and time of a story, which can also act as a social map.
Old money and new money: wealth long held by a family against wealth recently made.
Image: a concrete detail, such as a light or a house, that carries meaning beyond itself.
