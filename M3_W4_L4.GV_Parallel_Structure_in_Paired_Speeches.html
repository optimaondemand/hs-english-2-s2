Parallel Structure in Paired Speeches
Grammar + Vocab · Week 4
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Matching ideas take matching form.
Parallel structure puts items in a series, or the halves of a pair, in the same grammatical pattern. The speeches of Act 4 use it to build lists, balance losses, and answer one speaker with another.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify parallel structure in a series and in a pair.
2
I can revise a sentence with faulty parallelism.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · PARALLEL STRUCTURE

Parallel structure means giving matching ideas matching grammatical form. Items in a series, or two halves of a pair, share one pattern: verb phrase with verb phrase, noun with noun, clause with clause. The match makes the ideas read as equals and gives a speech rhythm. A break in the pattern makes a sentence hard to follow or comic.

✎ ON THE BOARD · THE PATTERN IN THE PLAY’S OWN SPEECHES
Demetrius: “Now I do wish it, love it, long for it,”
A series of three verb phrases, each a verb plus it or a phrase ending in it. The repetition builds a rising list.
Egeus: “You of your wife, and me of my consent,”
A pair. Each half uses the same pattern: pronoun, of, your or my, then the thing lost. The balance puts his loss beside Demetrius’s.
Oberon: “take hands with me, / And rock the ground whereon these sleepers be.”
Two commands joined by and, each starting with a verb. The matching form makes the pair read as one gesture.
Hermia, then Helena: “Methinks I see these things with parted eye,” / “So methinks.”
Paired speeches. Helena answers with Hermia’s opening verb, so her reply echoes the structure of the first.
Faulty: Demetrius wishes to marry Helena, to love her, and she is the one he longs for.
The last item breaks the pattern: two infinitives, then a clause.
Revised: Demetrius wishes to marry Helena, to love her, and to long for no one else.
Three infinitives. Parallel structure is restored.

Parallel structure applies to every item in a series and to both sides of a comparison. Paired speeches carry the pattern across two speakers. When one speaker reuses another’s key words, the audience hears agreement.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

The six words below come from Act 4, Scenes 1 and 2. Each is given in the sense the scene uses.

amity (noun) friendly relations after a quarrel
Root: Old French amitie, from Latin amicus, “friend.”
In the text: “Now thou and I are new in amity,” (Oberon)
enmity (noun) deep, lasting hostility
Root: Latin inimicus (in-, “not,” plus amicus, “friend”). Enmity is the opposite of amity.
In the text: “To sleep by hate, and fear no enmity?” (Theseus)
concord (noun) agreement; harmony
Root: Latin concordia, from con-, “together,” and cor, “heart.”
In the text: “How comes this gentle concord in the world,” (Theseus)
overbear (verb) to override; to prevail against
Root: Old English ofer, “over,” plus beran, “carry”: to bear down upon.
In the text: “Egeus, I will overbear your will;” (Theseus)
betrothed (adjective) formally promised in marriage
Root: Middle English bitreuthen: be- plus treuth, “pledge, truth.” Related to troth and truth.
In the text: “Was I betroth’d ere I saw Hermia.” (Demetrius)
repair (verb) to go; to return to a place
Root: Late Latin repatriare, “return to one’s country,” through Old French repairer. A different word from repair, “to mend.”
In the text: “May all to Athens back again repair,” (Oberon)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH
denouement (from French dénouement)
Meaning: the closing part of a plot, in which the tangles are resolved. In French, dénouement is “an untying,” from dénouer, “to untie,” from Latin nodus, “knot.”
Example: “In the denouement, the lovers are matched correctly and the quarrel in the wood is settled.”
