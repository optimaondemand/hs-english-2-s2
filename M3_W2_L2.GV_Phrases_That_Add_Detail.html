📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A short sentence can carry a great deal of detail when phrases are attached to its nouns and verbs.
Oberon’s description of the bank where Titania sleeps, like his account of the magic flower, is built largely from two kinds of phrase: prepositional and participial. Each one adds a layer to a single picture.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify prepositional phrases and say what each one tells the reader.
2
I can identify participial phrases and name the noun each one describes.
3
I can use both kinds of phrase to add detail to a short sentence.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · TWO KINDS OF PHRASE

A prepositional phrase begins with a preposition (in, of, with, between, on) and ends with its object. It tells where, when, how, or which one.

A participial phrase begins with a participle, a verb form ending in -ing or -ed (or an irregular form such as laid) that works as an adjective. It describes a noun, and it can contain prepositional phrases of its own.

To find a phrase, locate the noun it describes, and ask what the phrase adds. In the board below, each line shows the phrases Oberon uses to build one picture from the plain core of a sentence.

✎ ON THE BOARD · DETAIL ADDED BY PHRASES
Without the phrases (not Shakespeare’s wording): Titania sleeps.
There sleeps Titania sometime of the night, / Lull’d in these flowers with dances and delight;
of the night: prepositional phrase, tells when. Lull’d in these flowers with dances and delight: participial phrase describing Titania; it contains two more prepositional phrases, in these flowers (where) and with dances and delight (how).
Quite over-canopied with luscious woodbine,
over-canopied is a participle describing the bank. with luscious woodbine is a prepositional phrase that says what covers it.
The juice of it on sleeping eyelids laid
of it: prepositional phrase describing juice. on sleeping eyelids laid: participial phrase, also describing juice.
Flying between the cold moon and the earth, / Cupid all arm’d:
Flying between the cold moon and the earth: participial phrase describing Cupid; between the cold moon and the earth is the prepositional phrase inside it.

Notice that the phrases are placed close to the nouns they describe. A participial phrase that drifts from its noun can describe the wrong thing, so the writer keeps them together.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE PHRASE DO?
For each line, choose the reading that fits the grammar.
1. In “Lull’d in these flowers with dances and delight,” what does Lull’d in these flowers with dances and delight describe?
2. Which phrase in “The juice of it on sleeping eyelids laid” is a participial phrase?
3. “Quite over-canopied with luscious woodbine” describes a bank. Which part is the prepositional phrase?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from this week’s scenes, where nature and disorder are the subject. Each entry gives the sense the play uses.

contagious (adjective) · spreading by contact; carrying harm from one thing to another
Root: Latin contagiosus, from con- (together) + tangere (touch)
Contagious fogs; which, falling in the land,
distemperature (noun) · a disordered state; an upset in the balance of weather or body
Root: Latin dis- (apart) + temperare (to mix in due proportion)
And thorough this distemperature we see
dissension (noun) · disagreement that divides a group
Root: Latin dissentire, to differ in feeling, from dis- + sentire (feel)
From our debate, from our dissension;
progeny (noun) · offspring; the results that come from a source
Root: Latin progenies, from progignere (to beget)
And this same progeny of evils comes
anoint (verb) · to smear or rub with oil or liquid
Root: Latin inunguere, from in- (on) + unguere (to smear)
With a disdainful youth. Anoint his eyes;
chaplet (noun) · a wreath worn on the head
Root: Old French chapelet, a small headdress, from chapel, from Latin cappa (hood)
An odorous chaplet of sweet summer buds
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

Theater and writing borrow this French word for a scene in which people are arranged and held still, like a painting.

tableau (French: “picture”)
At the close of Act 3, the four lovers lie asleep on the ground, a quiet tableau after the quarrels of the night.
🔑 KEY TERMS
prepositional phrase: a preposition, its object, and any words that describe the object.
participle: a verb form ending in -ing or -ed, or an irregular form, used as an adjective.
participial phrase: a participle with the words that go with it, describing a noun.
