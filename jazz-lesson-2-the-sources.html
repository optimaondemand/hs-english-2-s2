From broad to focused
Change one thing at a time
●
A broad question has too many answers to search for. A focused one names a group, a time, and something to compare or measure. Narrow yours by changing one of those three, then check whether a source could answer it.
A MODEL LADDER, ON A DIFFERENT QUESTION
BROADWas the American dream real in the 1920s?
NARROWERWho could pursue the American dream in the 1920s, and who was kept out?
FOCUSEDHow did immigration law in the 1920s change who could pursue the dream?
Each step adds a group, a time, or a point of comparison. Replace the example with your own question.

2
Three formats, three kinds of evidence
What each shows and what each cannot
●
Different formats show different things. A written source can give reasons and context. A photograph or recording can show how a person or place looked or sounded at one moment. A table of counts can show how many, and how much it changed. No single format shows everything. A good feature knows which is which.
WHAT EACH FORMAT SHOWS, AND WHAT IT CANNOT
FORMATSHOWS BESTCANNOT SHOW
Written (article, essay, letter)Reasons, motives, and contextHow many people were affected
Visual or oral (photograph, recording)How a person or place looked or sounded at one momentWhat happened before or after, or what lay outside the frame
Quantitative (table, chart, census count)How many, how much, and change over timeWhy people made their choices
WHERE TO LOOK
Library of Congress, for photographs and recordings: loc.gov. U.S. Census Bureau, for tables and counts: census.gov. JSTOR, for scholarly articles, is in the next tab. Search your topic, open the item, and read its own description before you decide it is credible.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
A census table shows that a city’s population doubled in ten years. What can the table not show?

Getting into JSTOR
A school login and a search that works

JStor is a large digital library of scholarly journal articles across the humanities and sciences, including classics and literary studies. Optima Academy Online provides a shared school login so every student can search it directly.

🔑 SCHOOL LOGIN FOR JSTOR.ORG
Username: optimaonline  ·  Password: wisdom359
This is the same shared login every Optima student uses to access JStor. Go to jstor.org, sign in, and you can search and read articles directly.

Once you are signed in, search terms matter as much as the login itself. A vague search returns thousands of results with no clear direction. Build your terms from your narrowed question. Try terms like these:

“Great Gatsby American dream”
“Fitzgerald Jazz Age”
“Gatsby wealth class”

If your first search returns too much, narrow it (add a decade or a name). If it returns too little, broaden it. Searching well usually takes more than one try. Run each result through the credibility checks you used in Module 2 before you rely on it.

⚠️ WORTH KNOWING · AI AND CITATIONS
An AI tool can produce a citation that looks real for an article that does not exist. Open every source you plan to cite, and confirm that the title, author, and journal match what you wrote down.
7
Cite, paraphrase, and claim
Three moves with one source
●
A paraphrase restates the idea in your own words and keeps its meaning. A quotation uses the writer’s exact words, in quotation marks. In a formal register, you avoid slang, contractions, and “I think.” The citation gives a reader enough to find the source again.
MLA: THE NOVEL (A REAL, CHECKED ENTRY)
Fitzgerald, F. Scott. The Great Gatsby. Project Gutenberg, 17 Jan. 2021, www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/64317.
MLA: A JOURNAL ARTICLE FROM JSTOR (THE SHAPE ONLY)
Author Last, First. “Article Title.” Journal Name, vol. #, no. #, year, pp. #-#. JSTOR, www.jstor.org/stable/#######.
IN-TEXT FORMS
The novel, which has no page numbers in this edition: (Fitzgerald, ch. 3). Your JSTOR article: (Author 220). Do not invent a page number you did not see.

QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED

THE ASSIGNMENT

Choose one JSTOR article or another credible source you have opened and read. Paraphrase its key idea, write its MLA citation, and use the idea to support one claim of your own about your question, in a formal tone. Total length: 250 to 350 words.

●
Focus question. What does this source establish, and how far can it carry a claim of your own?
STUDENT DIRECTIONS
1
Search for a source on your question, open it, and read enough to state its main argument. Do not cite a source you have not opened.
2
Write the source’s MLA citation in the form shown in this lesson.
3
Paraphrase the source’s key idea in your own words, with no copied strings of words.
4
State one claim of your own that the source helps support, and explain how the source supports it and where its support stops.
5
Keep a formal register: no slang, no contractions, no “I think.” Use at least two of this week’s Word Study words, used accurately.
SUCCESS CRITERIA (YOUR CHECKLIST)
☐
The MLA citation names a real source that opens and is in correct form.
☐
The paraphrase restates the key idea faithfully in the writer’s own words.
☐
One claim is stated clearly, and the source is used to support it with an honest note on its limits.
☐
The tone is formal, at least two Word Study words are used accurately, and the writing is 250 to 350 words.
HOW IT’S SCORED
Criteria Full credit Partial Pts
Citation and faithful paraphrase
4 pts		 A real source is cited correctly in MLA form, and its key idea is restated faithfully in the writer’s own words. The citation has errors, the source is not verifiable, or the paraphrase copies wording or drifts from the source. 4
Claim supported in formal tone
4 pts		 One clear claim is supported by the source, with a note on the limits of that support, in a formal register. The claim is unclear, the source does not actually support it, or the tone is casual. 4
