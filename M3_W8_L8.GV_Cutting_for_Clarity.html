Cutting for Clarity
Grammar + Word Study · Week 8
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A sentence is finished when nothing more can be cut without losing meaning.
Wordiness hides the point. Three kinds of waste account for most of it: repeated meaning, a phrase where a word would do, and an opener that says nothing. Cutting is not shortening for its own sake; the evidence stays.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can cut redundant words and phrases from a sentence without changing its meaning.
2
I can replace a wordy phrase with a single word.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THREE KINDS OF WASTE

Cutting works in three moves. First, remove repeated meaning (a list of three verbs that mean the same thing). Second, replace a phrase with a word (in the event that becomes if). Third, delete an opener that adds nothing (it is important to note that). A final test: read the sentence aloud, then cross out each word in turn. If nothing is lost, the word goes.

✎ ON THE BOARD · BEFORE AND AFTER
I am to entreat you, request you, and desire you, to con them by tomorrow night
Redundancy: entreat, request, and desire carry one meaning. Quince asks three times for one thing.
Learn your parts by tomorrow night.
Revised. One verb, one action. The meaning survives intact.
All that I have to say, is to tell you that the lantern is the moon; I the man i’ the moon; this thorn-bush my thorn-bush; and this dog my dog.
Redundancy and throat-clearing: Moonshine announces what the audience can already see, and the court objects.
I am the man in the moon, with my lantern, thorn-bush, and dog.
Revised. Thirteen words for thirty-one, with every prop still named.
In the event that Theseus had chosen to enforce the law, Hermia would have been faced with the possibility of death.
Wordy phrases: in the event that, would have been faced with, the possibility of. Expository prose.
Had Theseus enforced the law, Hermia could have died.
Revised. A phrase becomes a word each time.
It is important to note that Titania’s speech describes disordered seasons.
An empty opener. The reader needs the claim, not a warning that a claim follows.
Titania’s speech describes disordered seasons.
Revised. The opener is deleted.

Cutting never trades precision for speed. The Prologue in Act 5 is not wordy; he is tangled, and cutting words would not mend him. Fixing a tangled sentence takes rewriting, not trimming. Cut only what repeats or adds nothing.

📚 PRACTICE · CUT THE SENTENCE

Cut each sentence. A model revision opens below each item.

Due to the fact that the lovers are unable to see clearly, the confusion in the wood continues on.
Model: Because the lovers cannot see clearly, the confusion in the wood continues.
Helena is a character who is very persistent and who pursues Demetrius with great persistence.
Model: Helena pursues Demetrius with great persistence.
It should be noted that Oberon’s blessing is a blessing of the house and of all who live in it.
Model: Oberon’s blessing covers the house and everyone in it.
⚡ SELF-CHECK
Choose the best cut in each case.
1. Which revision cuts “The reason that Titania is angry is because Oberon demands her boy” without losing meaning?
2. Which sentence is already concise and should be left alone?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words appear in this week’s review of the whole play. Learn the sense the play gives each word.

solemnity (noun): a formal, ceremonial occasion; a serious ritual observance
Root: Latin sollemnis, “established, ceremonial”. In the play: A fortnight hold we this solemnity In nightly revels and new jollity.
discretion (noun): good judgment; the ability to see what is wise to do or say
Root: Latin discretio, “separation, discernment”. In the play: True; and a goose for his discretion.
tedious (adjective): tiresome because of length or dullness
Root: Latin taediosus, from taedium (weariness). In the play: Merry and tragical? Tedious and brief?
valour (noun): great courage in the face of danger
Root: Latin valor, from valere (to be strong). In the play: This lion is a very fox for his valour.
premeditated (adjective): thought out and planned in advance
Root: Latin prae- (before) and meditari (to think over). In the play: great clerks have purposed To greet me with premeditated welcomes;
audacious (adjective): boldly daring, sometimes to the point of rudeness
Root: Latin audax, from audere (to dare). In the play: Of saucy and audacious eloquence.
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

Short Latin phrases survive in English because they compress a longer idea into a few syllables.

vice versa (from Latin, “the position being reversed”): the other way around; with the order changed
The phrase compresses a whole clause into two words. Example: “The court laughs at the players, and, vice versa, the players watch the court with dread.”
