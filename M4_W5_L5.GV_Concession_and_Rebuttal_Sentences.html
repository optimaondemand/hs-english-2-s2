Concession and Rebuttal Sentences
Grammar + Vocab · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A concession grants part of the other side’s point, and the rebuttal that follows is harder to dismiss.
Subordinating words such as although and while, and the pair granted that ... yet, build a concession and a rebuttal into one sentence.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can write a sentence that concedes a point and then rebuts it.
2
I can punctuate concession sentences correctly, including the comma and the choice between although, while, and yet.
3
I can use this week’s Word Study words accurately.
📚 PART 1 · CONCESSION AND REBUTTAL

A concession clause begins with a subordinating word and cannot stand alone. The rebuttal is the main clause. Real examples from this week’s reading follow.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CONCESSION, THEN REBUTTAL
Although, by the terms, as you propose, you take four openings and closes, to my three, I accede, and thus close the arrangement. (Lincoln, in a letter arranging the debates)
Concession: Douglas gets four openings and closes to Lincoln’s three. Rebuttal: Lincoln agrees anyway. The comma closes the concession clause.
I agree with Judge Douglas he is not my equal in many respects, certainly not in color, perhaps not in moral or intellectual endowment. But in the right to eat the bread, without the leave of anybody else, which his own hand earns, he is my equal, and the equal of Judge Douglas, and the equal of every living man.
Two sentences: a concession, then a rebuttal that begins with But. The rebuttal carries the point.
Angry as I was, as we all were, I was tempted to laugh whenever he opened his mouth.
The concession is a reduced clause (Angry as I was). It grants the anger before reporting the laughter.
“I did love him once—but I loved you too.”
A concession and a rebuttal joined by a dash and but, in speech.

Three patterns do the same work. Each pattern has a concession slot and a rebuttal slot.

✎ PATTERNS TO COPY
Although [concession], [rebuttal].
Comma after the concession clause. Never add “but” in the main clause after “although.”
While [concession], [rebuttal].
“While” here means “although,” not “at the same time as.”
Granted that [concession], [rebuttal].
Some writers add “yet” before the rebuttal: Granted that Gatsby’s parties are generous, yet they serve a single guest.

Two common errors: a fragment (Although Tom defends family values.) and a doubled connector (Although Tom defends family values, but he keeps a mistress.).

⚡ TRY IT · BUILD THE SENTENCE
Choose the correct sentence.
1. Which sentence is built and punctuated correctly?
2. In which sentence does “while” signal a concession?
3. Which opening best fits a rebuttal that begins with “yet”?
📚 WORD STUDY

Six words from this week’s reading and sources. Each entry gives the sense the passage needs.

hypocrisy (noun) · claiming principles one does not follow
Root: Greek hypokrisis, “acting on a stage”
In the text: for revolting barbarity and shameless hypocrisy, America reigns without a rival (Douglass)
equivocate (verb) · to speak ambiguously in order to avoid a clear statement
Root: Latin aequi-, “equal,” + vocare, “to call”
In the text: I will not equivocate; I will not excuse; (Douglass)
specious (adjective) · seeming reasonable but false
Root: Latin speciosus, “fair-looking,” from species, “appearance”
In the text: a specious and fantastic arrangement of words (Lincoln)
obdurate (adjective) · stubbornly hard-hearted
Root: Latin obdurare, “to harden”
In the text: Who so obdurate and dead to the claims of gratitude (Douglass)
sacrilegious (adjective) · violating something held sacred
Root: Latin sacrilegus, “temple robber”: sacra, “sacred things,” + legere, “to gather, steal”
In the text: sacrilegious irony (Douglass)
indiscreet (adjective) · lacking tact or caution
Root: Latin in-, “not,” + discretus, “separated, distinguished”
In the text: “She’s got an indiscreet voice,” I remarked. (Chapter 7)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN
status quo (Latin, “the state in which”)
Meaning: the existing state of affairs
In a sentence: A reformer challenges the status quo; a defender of the status quo points to what the present arrangement protects.
