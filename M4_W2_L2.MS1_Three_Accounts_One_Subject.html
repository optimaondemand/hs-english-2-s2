📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
One subject, three formats, three different accounts. Each format makes some things easy to see and others impossible to say.
This lesson compares a poem, a photograph, and a table of numbers on one subject: immigration to the United States in the early twentieth century. The aim is to read each format for what it can and cannot establish.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state what a written account, a photograph, and a table each make easy to see.
2
I can identify what each format cannot say and what it leaves out.
3
I can decide which account to trust on a given question, and on what grounds.
📚 SOURCES

Text. Emma Lazarus, “The New Colossus,” 1883, public domain. Lines are quoted below.

Visual. A Library of Congress photograph record, Immigrants at Ellis Island, Bain News Service, dated between 1907 and 1917, at loc.gov/item/97501640. Open the record to view the photograph. A photograph is the product of a photographer, who chose the moment and the frame.

Numbers. The U.S. Census Bureau, Historical Statistics of the United States, Colonial Times to 1970 (1975), whose Chapter C is titled Migration, at census.gov. A table of this kind counts people in categories that officials chose.

📚 PART 1 · THE SUBJECT AND ITS DATES

In 1921 and 1924, Congress passed laws that limited immigration by national origin. Before the laws, the United States admitted newcomers in large numbers, and a public argument followed about whether it should continue. The three accounts above enter that argument at different moments. The poem dates from 1883, the photograph from before 1917, and the tables cover many years. The first finding is that no single account is a picture of the 1920s, and each has to be placed in time before it is used.

A text can state a view and attribute it. Lazarus gives the speech to the statue:

“Keep, ancient lands, your storied pomp!” cries she
With silent lips. “Give me your tired, your poor,
Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free,
The wretched refuse of your teeming shore.
Send these, the homeless, tempest-tost to me,
I lift my lamp beside the golden door!”
Lazarus, The New Colossus, 1883 (public domain)

📚 PART 2 · FOUR QUESTIONS FOR ANY FORMAT
📝 WHAT EACH FORMAT CAN AND CANNOT SAY ABOUT ONE SUBJECT
THE POEM (TEXT)
Easy to see: an attitude and an ideal, stated, qualified, and attributed to a speaker.
Cannot say: how many arrived, who they were, what happened next.
Emphasizes and omits: welcome; omits restriction, cost, and who might be turned away.
THE PHOTOGRAPH (VISUAL)
Easy to see: faces, clothing, crowding, setting, at one moment.
Cannot say: names, reasons, what the people felt, what came before or after. It cannot qualify itself.
Emphasizes and omits: what is inside the frame; everything outside it is omitted. The choice of frame is an argument the picture never states.
THE TABLE (QUANTITATIVE)
Easy to see: scale and change over time, side by side.
Cannot say: why numbers changed, or what any person’s life was like.
Emphasizes and omits: whatever officials chose to count and how they sorted people into categories; anyone not counted is absent.

Four questions apply to any account of any subject:

1. What does this format make easy to see?
2. What can it not say at all?
3. Which details does it emphasize, and which does it omit?
4. Where it conflicts with another account, which is more reliable on this question, and why?

A table looks like the least interpretive format and is often the most interpretive in its choices, because someone decided what to count, whom to count, and what to leave uncounted. A photograph looks like a record and is a selection. A poem looks like opinion, and its opinion is open to view, which is a form of honesty the other two formats do not offer.

📚 PART 3 · WHERE THE ACCOUNTS CONFLICT

The poem promises welcome to “the homeless, tempest-tost” without conditions. The laws of 1921 and 1924 set conditions by national origin. The two do not describe the same country, and the question of which to trust depends on the question asked.

For what the nation said it wished to be, the poem is the better source. For what the law did, the laws and the tables of counts are better. For what the arrivals looked like and how they were received at a given door, the photograph offers a moment and no more. No single account answers all three questions, and the careful reader keeps all three formats in hand.

The novel holds these tensions too. Tom Buchanan’s remark at dinner belongs to the debate that produced the laws:

The idea is if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged.
Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Chapter 1 (public domain)

A reader who has compared three formats can say what that line shows (a speaker’s fear), what it cannot show (the actual population), and what source would test it (a table).

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH FORMAT SUPPORTS THE CLAIM
Choose the best answer for each question.
1. A table lists the number of immigrants admitted each year from several countries. Which conclusion does the table itself support?
2. A writer cites a photograph of arriving immigrants as proof that they were treated kindly. What is the main problem?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Frame: the edge of a photograph or other image, which decides what is shown and what is left out.
Quantitative source: a source built from counted data, such as a table.
Omission: a detail an account leaves out, which shapes its meaning as surely as what it includes.
